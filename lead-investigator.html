Tealc: the lab's own agent

Tealc is our in-house AI agent. It runs locally on Claude plus LangGraph, keeps persistent memory across sessions, and has access to the lab's email, calendar, drive, and research tools. The goal is not automation for its own sake. Working scientists accumulate enormous amounts of lower-level knowledge work: literature triage, email, scheduling, tracking student progress, formatting, summarizing. That load crowds out the things that actually require a trained scientist: thinking carefully about a problem, designing a study, and being present for mentees. Tealc is built to absorb that lower tier and hand back the attention it consumes. At its best it works the way a trusted right hand does, handling routine tasks without being asked and surfacing things worth noticing. How far that can extend, how much independent judgment and autonomous work it can take on, is the experiment we are running.

What's running now

Tealc runs as two coordinated processes sharing a single SQLite database. The reactive chat handles direct requests: email, calendar, literature, documents, data analysis. The background scheduler runs independently around the clock, surfacing work-ready briefings when the chat opens so the first message of the day is not a prompt but an update.

Admin, literature, and documents Email, calendar, Drive, Docs, Sheets, files, notes Reads, drafts, writes back. Sends nothing without approval.

Tealc reads Gmail with keyword filters, manages the calendar (read and write, no invitations sent without explicit approval), searches Google Drive by content, and reads local files including .docx and PDF manuscripts. It drafts reply emails that sit in Gmail waiting for a human to approve. It writes directly into Google Docs and can read and update Google Sheets, including the lab's karyotype databases and CURE data. A notes table in SQLite survives across sessions and machines, so context from one conversation carries into the next.

The research tools cover Europe PMC, bioRxiv, and OpenAlex with citation counts and open-access links. A citation tracker monitors who is citing the lab's own work. Tealc can also load the full llms-full.txt snapshot of the lab website, so it starts every conversation with the lab's research program, active projects, and student roster already in context.

Always-on scheduler Morning briefing, grant radar, student pulse Work done before the PI sits down. Three background jobs, one shared database.

Every morning at 7:45 am a background job assembles new citations to the lab's work, overnight preprints in chromosome and sex-chromosome evolution, flagged emails, the day's calendar, and a countdown of the nearest grant deadlines. Claude synthesizes these into a structured briefing under 600 words; if anything is urgent it fires a desktop notification before the chat even opens. Every Monday at 6 am a grant radar job scans NSF and NIH RSS feeds, scores each opportunity against the lab's profile using a fast Claude model, and queues the high-fit entries for review. Every Sunday evening a student pulse job flags anyone with no recent contact or an overdue milestone. Every job is logged to a job-runs audit table and the results surface automatically when the chat opens.

R execution and student tracking Runs comparative analyses, tracks every student's milestones ape, phytools, geiger wired in. Milestones, interactions, and overdue flags in SQLite.

Tealc can execute R scripts in a sandboxed working directory, read the output, and interpret the results in the context of the analysis. The R environment has ape, phytools, geiger, diversitree, and tidyverse installed. Every run saves the script and output for reproducibility. For student management, a SQLite database tracks each lab member's role, milestones (qualifier, proposal, defense, paper submissions), and interaction history. Tealc checks this automatically whenever a student's name comes up in conversation and surfaces overdue items without being asked.

What we're building next

The parts of the job that require the most judgment are also the most time-consuming: reading a manuscript critically, structuring a grant, responding to reviewers, writing recommendation letters. The next phase wires Tealc into those workflows.

Phase 4 · next milestone Manuscript and grant machine Structured critique, journal targeting, response to reviewers.

Given a full draft manuscript, Tealc produces a structured critique: argument flow, methods gaps, framing for the target journal, and the reviews we should expect. Given a grant call and prior successful grants, it helps structure specific aims, significance, and innovation sections. Given a manuscript plus reviews, it drafts a point-by-point response. Tealc writes the first pass; a human supplies the judgment about what to keep.

Phase 5 Deeper lab management Recommendation letters, lab-meeting agendas, alumni tracking.

The milestone tracker is running, but the higher-judgment tasks remain: drafting recommendation letters from a student's CV and the PI's notes, building lab-meeting agendas from recent progress, and tracking what alumni go on to do after they leave. How alumni do is one of the real measures of a lab, and it is the kind of longitudinal signal that falls through the cracks of a busy schedule.

The deeper question

Each phase adds capability, but the underlying question stays the same: how much of the knowledge work that fills a scientist's day can be handled by an agent, and how far can its judgment be trusted? The roadmap is structured so that every phase answers a piece of that question with real use, not prototypes. Code execution tells us whether the agent can run and interpret a phylogenetic analysis without supervision. Document write-back tells us whether it can contribute to a manuscript in a way a collaborator would recognize as useful. The student and lab management phase tells us whether it can hold institutional memory the way a long-tenured lab manager would.

We are not trying to replace the thinking that makes science work. The goal is to recover the time and attention that lower-level knowledge work takes from people who trained for years to do something harder. Whether current models can actually do that, and where they fall short on the specific kind of reasoning evolutionary biology demands, is what we are here to find out.

The problem is not that scientists lack ambition. It is that the job has grown into something dominated by tasks that do not require the training it took to get here. Tealc is an attempt to fix that.

Design principles

  • Draft-only on email. Tealc never sends. A human approves every message.
  • Service protection. Every incoming ask gets evaluated against the goals of the lab. Requests that would grow service load without advancing the science get a polite decline drafted automatically.
  • Triage mode. When the day is already overloaded, Tealc asks for the single most important thing first instead of offering a menu.
  • Persistent memory. Every conversation and note survives restarts and new laptops.
  • Audit trail. Every tool call is visible in the Chainlit step display. Nothing Tealc does is hidden from the human it works with.
  • AI amplifies effort, humans supply judgment. The lab's own AI philosophy, applied here.

Where this is likely to break

If a Claude-powered agent could already run a complete evolutionary-biology project unassisted, someone would have shipped it. Instead, we expect specific failure modes, and we are instrumenting the agent to surface them.

  • Hypothesis selection under uncertainty. Agents are trained to complete tasks. Research is not a task. We expect Tealc to drift toward hypotheses that are easy to test with the data in front of it, rather than hypotheses most likely to be true.
  • Biological plausibility. Computing a statistic is easy. Noticing that the statistic describes something biologically impossible is the test. We want to see whether Tealc flags it or publishes the number anyway.
  • Novel-result discipline. Agents are trained to be confident. Evolutionary biology asks researchers to be precise about which part of a result is actually new. This is where the seams will show most in a first-draft manuscript.
  • Scope discipline. A graduate student learns, painfully, when a project is done. An agent without explicit structure does not. Watching where the scope escapes is probably the single most informative signal.

How to follow along

Every prompt, every response, and every generated artifact is logged. Tealc's current live work is visible on the live console: a status indicator and a feed of the most recent tool calls, refreshed every thirty seconds. When the build reaches a publishable milestone, a companion manuscript will describe the methodology and the agent's behavior, intended as a case study for anyone else thinking about putting an agent on a real research problem.

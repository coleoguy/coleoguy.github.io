The lead · Striking EEB paper: cryptic bat diversity and conservation phylogenetics

Bat DNA reveals a hidden conservation crisis

Mitochondrial sequences uncover 95 candidate lineages, but their identity and projected extinction risk need more precise tests.

Esquivel DA, Penagos A, Feijó A, Ramos Pereira MJ·Nature Communications, 03 Nov 2026·doi:10.1038/s41467-026-78393-0

Bat diversity may be hiding in the space between genetic lineages and named species. Diego Esquivel and colleagues assembled 12,813 mitochondrial DNA sequences from Neotropical bats, covering 191 recognized species across 65 genera. They built 63 phylogenies and applied genetic species-delimitation methods to flag 95 distinct lineages within 45 species complexes. A species complex is a set of closely related populations currently grouped under one name. These lineages could represent undescribed species, but a DNA barcode alone does not formally establish species boundaries. The candidates occur across all nine bat families sampled, rather than belonging to one unusual branch of the bat tree.

The team then combined distribution models, human landscape modification and climate scenarios to rank conservation priorities. Their analysis identified ten regional hotspots of hidden diversity and found substantial projected habitat losses for many species complexes by 2070. These are forecasts, not observed extinctions. Moreover, climate projections describe entire complexes, not the separate hidden lineages, whose precise ranges remain uncertain. Much of the evidence comes from one mitochondrial gene, and poorly sampled regions could contain diversity the study missed. The urgent conclusion is about research priorities: taxonomists need specimens, nuclear genetic evidence, acoustics and ecological data before these candidate lineages can be formally described and protected.

Where to pushCan independent nuclear loci, morphology and acoustic recordings confirm the proposed boundaries in the highest-priority bat complexes? A coordinated museum and field campaign could sample geographic gaps, estimate lineage-specific ranges and rerun extinction-risk models. Compare the conservation rankings before and after formal species delimitation to test whether mitochondrial-only assessments prioritize the right places.

Beyond EEB · A paper from outside evolution and ecology

Cancer cells share a survival signal after chemotherapy

Chen Y, Zhong Y, Tian X et al. Nature Cell Biology, published 3 November 2026. Original study.

Chemotherapy damages cancer-cell DNA, but that damage may help nearby tumor cells survive. Working with head and neck cancer models, Yang Chen and colleagues traced a distress signal from activated STING, an intracellular DNA sensor, to secretion of the inflammatory molecule TNF. TNF then activated the enzyme IKKβ in neighboring cells, which modified ATG9A and increased autophagy, the process cells use to recycle components. The team linked this pathway to drug resistance in experiments and found a corresponding association in patient samples. Blocking TNF improved treatment response in preclinical models; whether this helps patients remains untested.

Beyond biology · A result from outside biology

Deep conductivity may explain the ice giants' strange magnets

Yan C, Soderlund KM, Aurnou JM et al. Communications Physics, published 3 November 2026. Original study.

Uranus and Neptune have magnetic fields that are unusually tilted and complex compared with Earth's roughly bar-magnet-like field. Chi Yan and colleagues used computer simulations of electrically conducting fluids to investigate why. When electrical conductivity increased with depth, their models generated irregular magnetic patterns resembling features measured by Voyager 2. Models with uniform conductivity more often produced simpler, nearly axis-aligned fields. The simulations also predict changes in magnetic structure over time, although periods of relative stability are possible. These are model results, not new spacecraft measurements. Future observations could test whether the planets' fields actually vary as predicted.

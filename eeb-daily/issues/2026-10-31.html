The lead · Striking EEB paper: microbial virus-host coevolution and CRISPR turnover

Microbial immune memories turn over across ecosystems

A census of nearly 800 million CRISPR spacers finds rapid turnover and warns that a viral match does not always identify a true host.

Roux S, Neri U, Bushnell B, Fremin BJ, George NA, Gophna U, Hug LA, Camargo AP, Wu D, Ivanova N, Kyrpides NC, Eloe-Fadrosh EA·Nature Microbiology, 29 Oct 2026·doi:10.1038/s41564-026-02480-7

Microbes store short snippets of invading DNA in CRISPR arrays, molecular records that can guide defense against later viral attacks. Simon Roux and colleagues developed a method for extracting these 'spacers' from mixed-community DNA sequencing reads. They screened 467,018 publicly available metagenomes and recovered about 794 million spacers from 367,609 samples. The dataset contained roughly 410 million distinct sequences. Most were rare, and, among well-sampled repeat groups, most spacer sequences appeared in only one sample. In contrast, spacers that were common within a microbial population were more likely to persist across samples collected from the same location or host. The patterns suggest frequent acquisition, turnover, and selective retention.

Comparing spacers with viruses and plasmids yielded more than a billion sequence matches. These matches can reveal encounters with foreign DNA, but they are not straightforward host assignments. Some viruses matched immune records from distantly related microbes, especially viruses carrying diversity-generating retroelements that can modify proteins used to contact cells. The authors propose that viral DNA sometimes enters a non-host cell without completing infection, yet still contributes a spacer. That explanation remains inferential rather than experimentally established. Most well-supported virus matches implicated one microbial class. The dataset is also unevenly sampled, with many human gut metagenomes, limiting global generalizations.

Where to pushPair time-series long-read metagenomes with cultured phages and host panels to measure both spacer turnover and successful infection. Does a virus with a diversity-generating retroelement leave CRISPR memories in cells it cannot replicate inside? The answer would distinguish genome entry from host range and strengthen the ecological inference drawn from sequence matches.

Classic · A landmark paper, revisited

When brothers compete, daughters are worth more

W. D. Hamilton, Science 156, 477–488 (1967). Original paper.

Why are males and females commonly produced in similar numbers when one male could fertilize many females? In 1967 W. D. Hamilton used population-genetic theory to show why the answer changes when mating occurs among relatives. If brothers compete with one another for sisters in a small patch, mothers can increase their genetic contribution by producing a strongly female-biased brood, with enough sons to mate with the daughters. This is local mate competition, a departure from the usual one-to-one prediction. Work on parasitoid and fig wasps subsequently made these sex-ratio predictions a prominent test of adaptation.

Beyond biology · A result from outside biology

An electron beam carves oxides one atom thick

Gao M et al., Nature Materials (29 Oct 2026). Original paper.

Researchers used a focused electron beam to remove halogen atoms from layered metal oxyhalides, producing free-standing metal oxide sheets only one atomic layer thick. They demonstrated the approach with chromium oxide and extended it to erbium and bismuth oxides. Some chromium oxide sheets formed single-crystal regions larger than 10,000 square nanometres, and the team patterned structures at micrometre scales. Calculations predict that monolayer chromium oxide is stable and could show antiferromagnetic order below 230 kelvin, but that magnetic state was not measured here. The method could enable engineered stacks and interfaces for studying unusual electronic and optical behavior.

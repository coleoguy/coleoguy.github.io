The lead · Evolutionary developmental biology: ancestral embryonic organizers

The chordate body plan has deeper roots

Experiments in a sea anemone suggest that embryonic organizer logic predates the origin of bilaterian animals.

Mikula Mrstakova S, Biswas P, Sengupta R, Klymets H, Machacova S, Kozmik Z, Kozmikova I·Nature Communications, 06 Nov 2026·doi:10.1038/s41467-026-78735-y

Why do very different animals establish the main axes of their embryos using some of the same molecular signals? A new study compares the gastrula organizer, a region that helps pattern an early embryo, in the sea anemone Nematostella and chordates. The question is whether their similarities reflect a shared ancestral developmental program or independently assembled systems. The authors combine regulatory DNA tests across species with pathway perturbations, single-cell comparisons and transplantation experiments. This is a more demanding test of evolutionary homology than noticing that two animals express the same genes.

Regulatory sequences associated with Nematostella Bmp2/4 and Chordin expression also drove organizer-restricted activity in chordate embryos. Smad2/3 signaling was active in the anemone organizer and necessary for organizer-specific expression of those genes. Together these observations support the idea that core organizer logic existed before the split between cnidarians and bilaterians. The authors offer a model in which changes in organizer position and elongation of the body axis contributed to the origin of the chordate body plan. That evolutionary reconstruction remains an interpretation of the comparative evidence, not a direct observation of an ancestral embryo.

Where to pushTest which parts of this regulatory system remain interchangeable across a broader sample of cnidarians and bilaterians. Cross-species enhancer assays can reveal conserved inputs, but those inputs may operate in different cellular contexts. Pair regulatory tests with lineage tracing and perturbations to ask whether the proposed ancestral organizer also specified comparable cell fates.

Beyond EEB · A paper from outside evolution and ecology

Human B cells become an evolution machine

Bram et al., Nature Communications (2026). Research paper.

Antibody-producing B cells naturally mutate immunoglobulin genes at unusually high rates, allowing immune responses to explore many protein variants. Researchers adapted that mechanism into a platform called CODE-HB, which redirects somatic hypermutation toward an engineered, stable location outside the immunoglobulin genes in human B-cell lines. The system generates substitutions, insertions and deletions in proteins displayed on cell surfaces or produced inside cells. The team used it to evolve antibodies that neutralize avian influenza and viral escape variants. This is laboratory-directed evolution rather than a natural evolutionary history. Its broader promise is a reusable route to improving biological molecules directly in human cells.

Beyond biology · A result from outside biology

Trapped ions reproduce a quantum interference effect

Saner et al., Nature Physics (published 25 Sep 2026; reported 7 Nov 2026). Research paper.

A quantum experiment can simulate interactions that are hard to calculate on conventional computers. In research first published September 25 and newly reported November 7, physicists used trapped ions as a hybrid device: electronic states represented gauge fields and vibrations represented matter. They built a small loop and observed an Aharonov–Bohm interference effect, in which the motion of a particle depends on magnetic flux even when the available paths are otherwise equivalent. The result demonstrates the required matter-field coupling in a controlled simulator. It does not mean realistic particle physics calculations are already within reach, but it provides a building block for more ambitious simulations.

The lead · Genome-scale horizontal gene transfer and chromatin regulation

Algal chromosomes persist inside mammalian cells

A laboratory fusion experiment shows that host epigenetic regulation can help foreign eukaryotic DNA survive for many generations.

Aoki R, Nishihara H, Sakamoto T, et al., Matsunaga S·Nature Communications, 12 Nov 2026·doi:10.1038/s41467-026-78753-w

Horizontal gene transfer, the movement of DNA between species rather than from parent to offspring, is common in bacteria. But can an animal cell accommodate whole chromosomes from an evolutionarily distant eukaryote? Ryota Aoki and colleagues brought the tiny red alga Cyanidioschyzon merolae together with cultured Chinese hamster ovary cells. They used experimentally induced cell fusion, as well as a less efficient route involving cellular engulfment, to study how algal DNA entered mammalian nuclei. Live imaging followed the arrival of foreign DNA, while genome sequencing and chromosome imaging measured what survived.

Some hybrid cell lines retained more than 16 million bases of algal DNA, including chromosome-scale structures. Foreign material persisted through 95 generations without continued drug selection. Most algal genes remained silent; the imported DNA acquired repressive chromatin marks and increasing DNA methylation, chemical modifications associated with reduced gene activity. When the researchers disrupted the mammalian methylation enzyme DNMT3A, stable retention of transferred DNA decreased. That supports a role for host epigenetics in accommodating an unfamiliar genome. It does not establish that chromosome transfers routinely happen in wild animals, or that mammalian cells became photosynthetic. The striking result is the experimentally observed survival of foreign chromosomes, not a demonstrated natural evolutionary pathway.

Where to pushCould the same methylation-dependent retention occur when foreign DNA arrives without laboratory-induced fusion? A next step would compare engulfment, natural cell interactions and engineered fusion using matched donor chromosomes, then measure integration, loss and transcription over many generations. That would separate a plausible route for evolutionary innovation from a striking but specialized cell-culture phenomenon.

Beyond EEB · A paper from outside evolution and ecology

Blue light switches off cellular recycling

Mondal et al., Nature Cell Biology (12 Nov 2026). Research article.

Autophagy is the cellular recycling process that delivers worn-out components to lysosomes for breakdown. Studying its immediate effects has been difficult because drugs can act broadly and gene knockouts take time. Mondal and colleagues developed ASAP, a light-sensitive protein system that blocks the fusion of autophagosomes with lysosomes within five minutes of blue-light exposure. The effect can reverse after the light stops. Using rapid proteomic measurements, the team found that incomplete nuclear-pore protein complexes are normally cleared through autophagy. Without that clearance, these complexes accumulate in cytoplasmic processing bodies. This is a research tool demonstrated in experimental cells, not a clinical treatment.

History · From the history of science

Dayhoff turned protein sequences into evolutionary evidence

Brewer, Embryo Project Encyclopedia (24 Oct 2026). Historical biography.

Margaret Oakley Dayhoff (1925–1983) helped invent the field now called bioinformatics. Trained in quantum chemistry, she recognized that computers could turn the growing collection of protein sequences into evidence about shared ancestry. Working at the National Biomedical Research Foundation, she and collaborators published the first Atlas of Protein Sequence and Structure in 1965, gathering 65 sequences from the scientific literature. A one-letter code made amino acid sequences easier to store and compare. Her group also developed point accepted mutation, or PAM, matrices to quantify amino acid substitutions among related proteins. Those tools supported sequence alignment and evolutionary inference. Today's enormous sequence databases and computational comparisons descend from that commitment to organizing biological information for reuse.

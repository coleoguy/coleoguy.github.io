The lead · Genome biology and comparative genomics: opsin gene families

Red opsins keep turning green, and the same four sites do the work

A third, overlooked LWS paralog in gobies joins the list of lineages that rebuilt green vision from a red-sensitive gene, and the substitutions behind it are the ones primates used.

Cortesi F, Escobar-Camacho D, Sommer GM, Luehrmann M, Hidber F, Deupi X, Carleton KL, Musilova Z·Molecular Ecology, Oct 2026·doi:10.1111/mec.70592

Vertebrate colour vision runs on a handful of opsin gene families, and duplication followed by spectral tuning is the standard route to a new channel. Most teleosts carry one long-wavelength-sensitive gene, LWS1, and cover green with the RH2 family. A green-shifted LWS duplicate, LWS2, was known only from characins and osteoglossomorphs. The question for gene family evolution is whether such replacements are one-off accidents or a repeatable solution: does a lineage that swaps RH2 for a retuned LWS copy find the same molecular path?

Cortesi, Musilova and colleagues mined teleost genomes and sequenced retinal transcriptomes. Elopomorphs (eels and tarpons) turn out to retain LWS2 as well, and gobies carry a previously missed third paralog, LWS3, from a duplication in the teleost or percomorph ancestor. Structural modelling of twelve LWS proteins found convergent substitutions at four retinal-binding-pocket positions (214, 259, 261, 269) shared by teleost LWS2, goby LWS3 and human MWS, the primate duplicate that gave us green. In characins, mormyrids, gobies and primates the shifted copy has partly or wholly replaced RH2. Among gobies, lws3 complements lws1 in most species and dominates the opsin profile in sicydiines; in the whitebarred goby, lws1 and lws3 occupy separate members of the double cone, where rh2 sits in other fishes.

Where to pushConvergence at four sites argues for a tightly constrained tuning landscape, but the evidence is twelve modelled proteins and one in situ species. The test is a measured spectral peak for LWS3 and dated rh2 losses across the goby tree, which would turn a tidy pattern into an ordered sequence of gain, retuning and replacement.

Field notes · In the news

A model trained on four million fossils says three of the big five were slow

Nature news, 20 Oct 2026. nature.com/articles/d41586-026-03316-4

The big five mass extinctions anchor every paleobiology syllabus, and the standard picture is a cliff in diversity at each boundary. Nature reports a machine-learning model trained on close to four million fossil occurrences that reconstructs Phanerozoic diversity at finer resolution than range-through counts allow, and its verdict is that three of the five were drawn out, with diversity sliding for millions of years before the boundary rather than collapsing at it. Expect the argument to centre on the Signor-Lipps effect: incomplete sampling makes abrupt events look gradual, and a model trained on the record inherits its gaps unless it corrects for them.

The wider campus · News from the academy

NSF's acting director is leaving, and the Senate still has not moved on his successor

Clare Zhang, AIP FYI, 21 Oct 2026. aip.org/fyi

Brian Stone, acting director since Sethuraman Panchanathan resigned in April 2025, leaves NSF at the end of 2026 after 26 years; acting chief of staff Rebecca Keiser, its first chief of research security, leaves at the end of October. Jim O'Neill, nominated for director early this year, has waited in the Senate since March without a hearing. If he is not confirmed by January 1, the agency will choose a new acting director with the White House. NSF has lost more than a third of its staff since January 2025.

Why it mattersStone's tenure covered the GRFP eligibility changes, more than a thousand terminations and the new industry-partnered fellowships. The spring GRFP and CAREER cycles will run under an acting director the White House helps pick, with a nominee the Senate has never questioned.

The lead · Sex chromosome evolution: independent loss of X-based sex determination

Scale insects lost their X twice

New insect genomes reveal two distinct ways an ancestral X chromosome can lose its role in determining sex.

Mongue AJ, Powell EC, Liesenfelt T, Grebler E, Markee A·Molecular Biology and Evolution, 3 Nov 2026·doi:10.1093/molbev/msag275

Sex chromosomes are usually presented as stable landmarks, but scale insects repeatedly abandoned the familiar X-based system. Andrew Mongue and colleagues sequenced 17 scale insect species to track transitions among ordinary chromosomal sex determination, paternal genome elimination, and self-fertilizing hermaphroditism. Under paternal genome elimination, males inherit two parental chromosome sets but silence or discard the paternal contribution. Comparing chromosome assemblies and related genes across a reconstructed family tree, the researchers inferred two independent losses of X-linked sex determination. One occurred in the monophlebid lineage containing hermaphrodites. The other preceded a radiation of families using paternal genome elimination.

The genomic routes were strikingly different. Among monophlebids, rearrangements began before the transition, ultimately merging former X material with two unusually large autosomes in hermaphrodites. In some paternal-genome-elimination lineages, the ancestral X remained recognizable despite losing its sex-determining role. In others, including mealybugs, its pieces were extensively rearranged. Surprisingly few ancestral gene families disappeared across these transitions. Strong differences in expression between males and females also appeared to predate turnover. Those patterns are consistent with several possible evolutionary pathways, not proof that sex-biased expression caused the system to change. The work separates the loss of chromosome identity from the loss of a chromosome's reproductive function.

Where to pushTest whether the expression differences inferred before turnover actually predict which lineages abandon X-based determination. Add genomes and sex-specific expression data from close relatives bracketing each transition. Could sex-determination genes move without much gene loss, and would experimentally altering their expression recreate any part of the transition?

Beyond EEB · A paper from outside evolution and ecology

Fly-derived shells deliver gene editors to mouse muscle

Lash B, Strebinger D, Segel M et al. Nature Communications, 4 Nov 2026. Original article.

A protein descended from a fly transposable element has become an unexpected delivery vehicle for gene editing. Researchers assembled the Drosophila Arc1 protein into virus-like shells using purified components outside cells, then loaded them with RNA or Cas9 editing machinery. The particles entered mammalian cells through a receptor called SORCS2. When injected directly into the muscles of mice modeling Duchenne muscular dystrophy, the loaded shells produced up to 18% targeted exon skipping and restored some dystrophin protein. This is a proof of concept in mice, not a demonstrated treatment. Widespread delivery to muscle throughout the body remains a major obstacle.

History · From the history of science

Mealworm chromosomes revealed the X and Y

Nettie Stevens, Studies in Spermatogenesis (1905). Historical analysis, Arizona State University.

In 1905, Nettie Stevens examined dividing cells in the mealworm beetle and uncovered a physical explanation for inherited sex. Female cells carried twenty large chromosomes; male cells carried nineteen large ones and a small one. Eggs contributed a large chromosome, while sperm came in two types. Stevens reasoned that sperm carrying the smaller chromosome produced males, and sperm with its larger counterpart produced females. The pair later became known as X and Y. Her work at Bryn Mawr linked an observable chromosomal difference to a major biological trait, strengthening the emerging chromosome theory of inheritance. Edmund Beecher Wilson investigated related questions independently that year. Stevens died in 1912, but her evidence became foundational to modern genetics and sex-chromosome research.

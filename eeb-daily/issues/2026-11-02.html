The lead · Chromosome evolution: centromere drive and structural supergenes

A selfish centromere builds an oversized chromosome

Monkeyflower genome assemblies reveal how a driving chromosome gathered rearrangements, repeated DNA and extra genes without yet revealing which change makes it cheat.

Stark-Dykema E, Finseth FR, Conner W, Plott CB, Fishman L·Genetics, 31 Oct 2026·doi:10.1093/genetics/iyag311

A chromosome in the yellow monkeyflower can cheat Mendel's 50:50 inheritance rule. During female meiosis, only one of the resulting products ultimately contributes to an egg, giving selfish centromeres an opportunity to bias transmission. Evan Stark-Dykema and colleagues compared chromosome-scale genome assemblies representing three distinct haplotypes, or versions, of the meiotic drive region on chromosome 11. The driving version, called D, contains a centromere, carries fitness costs and nevertheless persists in wild populations. The new comparison asks how one selfish locus accumulated a large block of linked genetic changes.

Compared with nondriving chromosomes, D carries at least two major rearrangements, including an inversion that links arrays of centromeric satellite DNA while trapping more than 200 genes between them. Expansion of a particular repeat family makes the driving chromosome more than 50% longer than its counterparts. A roughly two-megabase region unique to D also contains about 37 additional genes acquired through duplication and relocation. The authors identify expanded repeats as candidates for driving biased segregation and linked genes as possible modifiers of that advantage. But comparative assemblies cannot establish which change actually causes the distortion; testing causality will require experiments that separate the repeat expansions from neighboring genes.

Where to pushReconstruct the sequence of structural changes using additional driving and nondriving haplotypes from populations with different transmission biases. Then experimentally separate centromeric repeat expansions from candidate modifier genes. Which changes alter the fraction of eggs inheriting D, and which merely carry fitness costs? The proposed molecular mechanism remains to be demonstrated.

Beyond EEB · A paper from outside evolution and ecology

Oxytocin neurons help the body switch into motion

Vandendoren M et al. eLife 14:RP108212 (version of record, 30 Oct 2026). Primary paper.

Oxytocin is famous for social behavior, but a mouse study points to a more basic job: coordinating the switch from rest to activity. Vandendoren and colleagues monitored oxytocin-producing neurons in the hypothalamus while recording body temperature and behavior. Neural activity tracked transitions from cooler, quiet states toward heat production and arousal, even when mice were alone. Stimulating these neurons during low-temperature resting periods increased thermogenesis, followed by movement. The experiments support a role in changing physiological states, not simply social warmth. Reviewers note that the work does not yet isolate oxytocin release from possible co-released glutamate.

Beyond biology · A result from outside biology

Two black holes feed inside an early galaxy collision

Suh H et al. Nature Astronomy (online 30 Oct 2026). Primary paper.

Astronomers have identified two actively feeding supermassive black holes in a gas-rich collision from when the universe was about 1.3 billion years old. James Webb Space Telescope spectroscopy separated two bright galactic nuclei about 1.5 kiloparsecs apart in projection. Both show broad hydrogen emission associated with rapidly moving gas near growing black holes. Independent ALMA observations reveal corresponding cold-gas components, strengthening the merger interpretation. Published October 30, the study shows how dusty collisions might fuel early black-hole growth. The projected separation is not their true three-dimensional distance, and the observations cannot establish when the black holes will merge.

The lead · Sex chromosome regulation: dosage compensation and germline silencing

Sex chromosomes have no single regulatory solution

A new synthesis finds that the rules of dosage compensation and germline silencing vary widely across animals.

Mank JE, Schwander T·Evolution Letters, 04 Nov 2026·doi:10.1093/evlett/qrag043

Sex chromosomes often lose genes on the Y or W, leaving the other sex chromosome with an unequal dose. The standard explanation is that dosage compensation restores balanced gene expression. But the expression of sex-linked genes can also change during the formation of sperm or eggs, sometimes through broad silencing. Judith Mank and Tanja Schwander review these phenomena together in Evolution Letters. Their starting point is a striking comparative fact: animals have repeatedly evolved sex chromosomes, yet the resulting regulatory systems differ in which tissues they act, how strongly they alter expression and whether they target individual genes or an entire chromosome.

The review separates well-established observations from unresolved evolutionary explanations. Equal expression between sexes does not necessarily mean that expression is balanced between the X chromosome and the autosomes. Suppression of sex chromosome expression during meiosis may also have multiple causes and consequences, rather than one universal function. Existing regulatory machinery might be recruited for a newly formed sex chromosome, and could persist or change after chromosome turnover. These ideas offer testable hypotheses, not new experimental results: the article synthesizes studies across animals. The authors argue that comparing nonmodel species and coupling genomic measurements with direct functional experiments are essential to understanding why these systems differ.

Where to pushUse independent sex chromosome turnovers as natural replicates. In closely related species with different sex determining chromosomes, measure expression separately in somatic tissue and defined germ-cell stages. Test whether the same regulators are recruited to the new chromosome, and whether an old chromosome retains regulatory marks after losing its sex determining role.

Combining two cell models is not always better

Richter et al., Nature Machine Intelligence (2026). Research article.

A foundation model trained on cell gene expression and another trained on tissue images each capture useful biology. But what happens when their outputs are combined? Researchers compared ten methods of integrating these data across spatial transcriptomics datasets, where measurements are tied to tissue locations. They introduced a Synergistic Information Score to ask whether the combination reveals useful information beyond either input alone. Some tasks, such as predicting cell composition, were already well served by one modality. Others, including certain tissue niche classifications, benefited from methods that learned cross-modal interactions. The lesson is practical: multimodal AI needs complementary evidence, not merely more inputs.

Beyond biology · A result from outside biology

Interference makes a molecular state resist decay

Guthmann et al., Nature Physics (9 Nov 2026). Research paper.

An atom pair should normally break apart when its energy lies above the threshold for dissociation. Quantum interference offers a surprising exception: a bound state can remain localized inside a sea of scattering states. Researchers created this situation with ultracold lithium-6 atoms, using a periodically driven field to couple two tunable collision resonances. At a particular setting, two pathways interfered destructively, canceling the coupling that would otherwise let the molecular state decay. The atoms then became effectively transparent in collisions. This controlled realization of a bound state in the continuum offers a way to engineer interactions and losses in quantum systems.

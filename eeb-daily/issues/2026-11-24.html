The lead · Evolutionary theory: host behavior and the evolution of virulence

Host movement changes the evolution of parasite harm

A theoretical study shows why accounting for host behavior changes predictions about the evolution of virulence.

Rodrigues AMM, Gardner A, Bailey NW·Evolution, 20 Nov 2026·doi:10.1093/evolut/qpag204

Why do parasites harm their hosts to different degrees? Virulence is the harm an infection causes, and evolutionary theory has long considered the tension between a parasite's immediate success and the costs of damaging its host. António Rodrigues and colleagues revisit this problem by asking whether host movement can be treated as independent of infection. That assumption simplifies the mathematics, but it overlooks a familiar feature of disease: sick animals often move differently from healthy ones. The authors develop a theoretical extension of existing evolutionary models that explicitly recognizes this connection between behavior and ecology.

Their analysis predicts that selection can favor parasites that cause less damage when infection limits host mobility. The authors identify changes in local population demography and genetic relatedness as important parts of the explanation. The work is a reminder that the selective environment is not necessarily fixed. A trait can change the surroundings in which its own evolutionary consequences play out, a process called eco-evolutionary feedback. This is a theoretical result rather than evidence that every parasite behaves this way in nature. Its broader significance is that behavioral effects of disease belong in the same conceptual picture as survival and reproduction.

Where to pushHow much of the apparent disagreement between studies of virulence comes from differences in what researchers mean by harm? A useful next step would be a synthesis of existing observational studies that separates behavioral effects from mortality, assesses the quality of the evidence, and identifies where the theoretical assumptions remain untested.

Classic · A landmark paper, revisited

Discrete genes explain continuous traits

R. A. Fisher, Transactions of the Royal Society of Edinburgh, 1918. Original paper.

A pea can be wrinkled or smooth, but human height spans a continuous range. How could both follow Mendel's rules? Fisher showed mathematically that variation at many individually inherited loci can generate continuous trait distributions. He also developed ways of partitioning variation into contributions from the environment, average effects of alleles, and dominance. His equations explained why different kinds of relatives resemble each other to different degrees. The resulting framework reconciled two competing traditions of genetics and became the foundation of quantitative genetics. A century later, genome-wide association studies could estimate many of the effects that Fisher had treated theoretically.

Beyond biology · A result from outside biology

Two quasars sit inside an enormous bubble of hot gas

Hall et al., Nature Astronomy, 18 Nov 2026. Paper.

Supermassive black holes do more than consume matter. When actively feeding, they can transfer energy to the gas surrounding their galaxies. Kirsten Hall and colleagues detected a bubble around two quasars from an era when the universe was only 2.8 billion years old. They used a distortion of the cosmic microwave background caused by its interaction with hot electrons to estimate the gas's thermal energy. The inferred energy is substantially larger than expected from gravitational heating alone. The observation supports the idea that quasar winds can suppress future star formation, although the study does not directly demonstrate that star formation has stopped.

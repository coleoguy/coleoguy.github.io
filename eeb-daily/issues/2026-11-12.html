The lead · Sex-specific macroevolution of lizard body size

Male and female lizards change size at different speeds

Separating male and female traits reveals evolutionary patterns that a single measure of sexual dimorphism misses.

Rodríguez-Rodríguez JD, Villalobos F, Gonzalez-Voyer A, Velasco JA·Evolution, 10 Nov 2026·doi:10.1093/evolut/qpag205

Male and female lizards can be strikingly different sizes, but the evolutionary history of that gap is not simply the history of body size. In a new Evolution study, Juan D. Rodríguez-Rodríguez and colleagues compared male and female body-size trajectories with the evolution of sexual size dimorphism, or SSD, across 2,476 squamate species. SSD is the difference in average size between the sexes. The researchers fitted evolutionary models for four diverse lizard families: anoles, chameleons, liolaemids and phrynosomatids. Rather than treating a species' size as one number, they followed size in each sex separately.

The analysis found that male body size generally evolved faster than female body size in three of the four focal families. Liolaemid lizards were the exception and showed the highest overall evolutionary rates. Yet male and female sizes had broadly similar patterns of disparity, meaning the spread of trait values across lineages, while disparity in SSD followed a different course. The contrasts also changed with the phylogenetic scale studied. These patterns caution against treating dimorphism as a simple proxy for selection on males or females. Comparative models identify differences in evolutionary history, not the forces that produced each change.

Where to pushCan the family-level pattern survive a model that allows different selective regimes in mountains, forests and deserts? A useful next test would pair sex-specific trait data with environmental histories and phylogenetic uncertainty, then ask where changes in male and female size actually precede shifts in dimorphism rather than simply accompany them.

Beyond EEB · A paper from outside evolution and ecology

Immune cells decide whether brown fat burns fuel

Song et al., Nature (11 Nov 2026). Research article.

Brown fat helps mammals stay warm by burning chemical energy as heat, but not all its cells work equally hard. Song and colleagues found that nearby immune cells, including neutrophils and macrophages, release a signal called IL-1β that keeps some brown fat cells in a low-heat state. Cold suppresses that signal and allows cells to switch on UCP1, a protein involved in heat production. In mice, removing a downstream glucocorticoid receptor preserved heat generation after cold exposure. Older mice with excessive immune signaling also regained thermogenic activity when that pathway was inhibited. This maps a biological switch, not a treatment tested in people.

Beyond biology · A result from outside biology

Uneven networks make materials absorb impacts

Both et al., Nature (11 Nov 2026). Research article.

Most engineered cellular materials repeat the same microscopic building block. Both and colleagues built a different kind: a network whose struts vary widely in length, thickness and connectedness, much like veins in leaves. Their two-parameter design generated physical test structures and computer models with adjustable stiffness across directions and deformation distributed away from a single weak spot. Under dynamic loading, selected networks also dissipated more energy than comparison architectures. One demonstration routed stress away from a vulnerable embedded object. These are experiments on engineered prototypes, not proof that a commercially useful helmet exists, but they offer a flexible way to design protective and wave-controlling structures.

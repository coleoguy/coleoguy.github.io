The lead · Population genetics: bottlenecks, genetic load and inbreeding

A bottleneck does not guarantee genetic recovery

New theory separates the loss of inbreeding depression from the harder recovery of population fitness.

López-Cortegano E, Charlesworth B·Genetics, 09 Nov 2026·doi:10.1093/genetics/iyag321

When a population suddenly shrinks, relatives are more likely to inherit the same variants, including harmful recessive mutations. These mutations can reduce survival or reproduction when both copies are harmful. Conservation geneticists therefore ask: does increased homozygosity expose damaging variants to selection strongly enough to remove them, and can average fitness eventually recover? Eugenio López-Cortegano and Brian Charlesworth address those questions with population genetic theory in Genetics. They consider a small, randomly mating population founded from a much larger population at mutation-selection-drift equilibrium, the long-term balance of mutation, selection and chance.

The authors combine computer simulations with approximate mathematical predictions for changes after the bottleneck. Their results show that purging can accelerate the loss of inbreeding depression, the extra fitness cost of mating between relatives. But an actual recovery in mean fitness requires mutations to be sufficiently recessive and selection to be strong enough relative to drift. Weakly harmful mutations instead produce declines in fitness and inbreeding depression broadly comparable to those expected from rising homozygosity under a neutral model, at least over the short periods studied. Reduced inbreeding depression, therefore, is not proof that a population has become genetically healthy. This is a theoretical result, not evidence that bottlenecked wild populations inevitably rebound.

Where to pushExtend the framework to populations whose sizes rebound after a crash, with linked loci and nonrandom mating. Across simulations, ask whether the decline of inbreeding depression predicts absolute fitness recovery or merely loss of segregating variation. Then test these divergent forecasts against pedigreed conservation populations with multigenerational survival and reproductive data.

Beyond EEB · A paper from outside evolution and ecology

The brain's regions differ in how they combine information

Ruff et al., Nature Neuroscience (10 Nov 2026). Research article.

Two regions of the brain may carry the same information but use it differently. Ruff and colleagues recorded neuronal populations in two rhesus monkeys judging the direction of moving dots while considering potential juice rewards. Visual area MT represented motion and reward largely separately; dorsolateral prefrontal cortex combined them in overlapping population patterns. A recurrent neural network generated predictions for stimulation experiments. Stimulating MT shifted judgments toward the represented motion direction, while stimulation of prefrontal cortex more often produced all-or-none, winner-take-all choices. Reading information from neural activity, the authors argue, does not establish what computation that tissue performs.

Beyond biology · A result from outside biology

Tangled DNA makes a restless liquid beat in unison

Levanon et al., Nature Physics (09 Nov 2026). Research article.

Can a liquid of randomly swirling molecular motors organize itself into a coordinated rhythm? In a Nature Physics experiment, researchers combined microtubules and kinesin motors with long strands of DNA. Initially, the motors drove irregular fluid motion. The motion stretched and tangled the DNA, building an elastic network that then fed back on the flow. Above a threshold DNA length, the mixture developed synchronized vortices spanning roughly two millimeters and reversing on timescales of tens of minutes. Shorter DNA strands did not produce stable oscillations. The work reveals mechanical self-organization without a chemical timing circuit, suggesting design principles for responsive soft materials.

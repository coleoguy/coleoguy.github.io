The lead · Phylogenetic comparative methods and macroevolutionary rates

Water gives body size more room to shift

Across more than 21,000 vertebrates, inferred changes in favored body size were larger and more frequent in aquatic lineages than on land or in the air.

Pagel M, O'Donovan C, Meade A·Nature Communications, 27 Oct 2026·doi:10.1038/s41467-026-78289-z

Can lineages with different histories evolve body size in similar ways? Mark Pagel, Ciara O'Donovan and Andrew Meade compared more than 21,000 vertebrate species, including mammals, birds, reptiles, fishes and amphibians. Their analysis treats an adaptive peak, the body size favored by selection in an ecological setting, as something that may move gradually or occasionally shift more sharply. These peaks are inferred from trait patterns and evolutionary relationships; the researchers did not measure natural selection directly. They asked whether species living in water, on land or in the air show recurring differences in the scale and frequency of those inferred changes.

The answer followed ecology more than ancestry. Estimated shifts toward larger or smaller favored body sizes were largest and most frequent in aquatic species, intermediate in terrestrial species, and smallest and least frequent in flying species. That ordering recurred across major vertebrate groups despite their different evolutionary histories. Independent data on body-size variation within populations showed the same broad aquatic-to-aerial ranking, which fits the idea of different opportunities for evolutionary change. This does not mean every aquatic lineage evolves faster, or that aquatic environments directly caused the pattern. The central result concerns rare changes in statistically inferred adaptive peaks over deep time.

Where to pushA useful next test would model habitat transitions alongside differences in body size, sampling, evolutionary variance and uncertainty in phylogenetic trees. Does the aquatic advantage survive those controls? Within-species estimates of selection in aquatic and terrestrial relatives could reveal whether inferred shifts in favored size correspond to actual changes in selective pressures.

Beyond EEB · A paper from outside evolution and ecology

A map reveals unexpected neighbors on immune cells

Floyd BM et al. Nature Structural & Molecular Biology (2026). Original paper.

Cell membranes are often drawn as a scattered collection of receptors, but which proteins occupy the same neighborhoods? Brendan Floyd and colleagues used proximity labeling, which marks proteins near chosen targets, to map 85 antigens on human T cells and B lymphoblasts. Mass spectrometry identified nearby proteins, producing a map of cell-surface organization. Familiar groups, including the T cell receptor complex, emerged alongside unexpected clusters. The researchers also detected proteins typically associated with mitochondria at the cell surface. A machine-learning model identified the IL-10 receptor beta subunit as a regulator of type I interferon signaling, turning the map into testable biological predictions.

History · From the history of science

McClintock found genes that would not stay put

Cold Spring Harbor Laboratory Archives. Barbara McClintock collection and biography.

Before scientists could read DNA sequences, Barbara McClintock followed chromosome behavior and kernel colors in maize. She found genetic elements that could move to new chromosomal positions, changing how nearby genes behaved. She first noticed this unusual behavior in 1944, published key findings in 1950 and presented her theory of controlling elements at Cold Spring Harbor in 1951. Other geneticists respected her experiments but questioned the interpretation. Later molecular studies established that transposable elements also occur in organisms such as yeast, flies and humans. Earlier in her career, McClintock helped connect genetic crossing over with physical chromosome exchanges and described cycles of chromosome breakage and fusion. She received the 1983 Nobel Prize for the discovery of mobile genetic elements and died in 1992.

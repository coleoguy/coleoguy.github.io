The lead · Striking EEB paper: long-term asexuality and phylogenetic reconstruction

Ancient asexuality can scramble a family tree

A ladder-shaped planarian phylogeny reflects unusual biology.

Benítez-Álvarez L, Tenaguillo-Arriola I, Leria L, Fernández R, Riutort M·Systematic Biology, 24 Oct 2026·doi:10.1093/sysbio/syag079

A family tree usually groups individuals from the same population together. Earlier work on Dugesia, a genus of freshwater planarians, found something stranger: asexual individuals appeared separately on long branches, forming a ladder. These animals reproduce by fission, splitting into pieces that regenerate. The pattern raised a basic question. Had the reconstruction method been fooled, or had a long history without sexual reproduction changed the genetic signal itself? The answer matters because researchers routinely use branching trees to infer how populations and species are related.

Lisandra Benítez-Álvarez and colleagues revisited the problem using transcriptomes, which sample expressed genes, and nuclear markers. They compared phylogenetic analyses with genetic variation within individuals and examined explanations involving prolonged asexuality or hybridization. The authors report that the ladder persists as a biological signal. Comparisons with other fission-reproducing planarians support an ancient history, while retained ancestral genetic variants obscure the relationships that conventional trees try to recover. The study therefore connects a question about asexual survival to a practical problem in evolutionary inference: a tidy tree model can misrepresent a complicated genetic history.

Where to pushThe next step is to ask how reliably competing histories can be distinguished. Simulations that vary the age of asexuality, ancestral diversity and hybrid ancestry could reveal when a ladder is diagnostic and when it is ambiguous. That would turn an intriguing pattern into a test with known limits.

Beyond EEB · A paper from outside evolution and ecology

Local rules build a world of shells

Liu H, Bhattacharya K, PNAS, 2026, 10.1073/pnas.2614478123

Molluscs build an astonishing range of shells by adding material at the growing edge. Huan Liu and Kaushik Bhattacharya asked how a local growth rule could generate these entire shapes. Their model repeats a fixed rule that depends only on the edge's geometry. Mathematically, the resulting shell is traced by a Lie group, a continuous family of transformations, acting on the larval shell. The authors describe nearly all known shell forms with three ingredients: a scale, an orientation vector, and the initial edge curve. Relating these ingredients to a phylogeny gives a compact way to compare shell shapes and their evolution.

Beyond biology · A result from outside biology

One positron becomes a precision physics laboratory

Myers TG, Soucy L, Sukra BAD, Sinha B, Gabrielse G, Physical Review Letters, 2026, 10.1103/gbkc-6nlj; Viewpoint in Physics

A single positron, the electron's antimatter counterpart, can now be controlled one quantum jump at a time. Researchers at Northwestern University trapped a positron for months, cooled its motion, and detected changes in its cyclotron motion and spin without destroying it. The apparatus holds the particle inside a microwave cavity, which slows the decay of excited states enough to measure them. This is a platform for a future precision measurement, not that measurement itself. Comparing positron and electron magnetic moments could test CPT symmetry, which predicts equal magnitudes and opposite signs. The team opens a route to much sharper tests.

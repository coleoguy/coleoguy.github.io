The lead · Plant functional evolution: fern phloem anatomy

Fern sugar conduits narrowed through deep time

Living and fossil fern tissues reveal an evolutionary shift toward narrower sugar-transport cells, with consequences for flow still awaiting direct tests.

Fu Z, Cruz R, Jensen KH, Hetherington AJ·Nature Plants, 26 Oct 2026·doi:10.1038/s41477-026-02440-3

Ferns distribute sugars through phloem, a network of living conducting cells called sieve elements. Zishan Fu and colleagues examined thin sections from 26 living fern species in 21 families, collecting more than 18,000 anatomical measurements, then compared those data with eight fossil species. Sieve-element radius varied widely among living ferns, contradicting the traditional picture of comparatively uniform phloem. Closely related species tended to have similar cell dimensions. Radius also covaried with leaf area and vascular anatomy, linking the dimensions of transport tissue to the size and organization of the structures it serves. The authors used phylogenetic comparative analyses rather than treating all species as independent observations.

Fossils offered an independent view of direction through time. Across several comparisons, extinct ferns had wider sieve elements than living relatives. Reconstructions from living species also suggested a shift toward smaller cells in later-diverging lineages, particularly polypods. A fluid-flow model translates narrower tubes into lower potential sugar conductivity, but that is not a direct measurement of sap transport. Pressure gradients, sieve pores, and plant dimensions could alter realized flow. The study establishes an anatomical evolutionary pattern more firmly than its ecological explanation. Whether the rise of flowering-plant forests favored reduced transport capacity in fern understories remains a hypothesis, not a demonstrated cause.

Where to pushMeasure phloem flow in living fern species across the inferred radius gradient, then test whether pore geometry, viscosity and source-to-sink distance change the predicted relationship. Compare models that account explicitly for phylogenetic uncertainty and fossil preservation. Could the apparent long-term trend reflect changing fern body size rather than direct selection on sugar transport?

Classic · A landmark paper, revisited

Weak selection can lose to chance

Ohta T. Slightly deleterious mutant substitutions in evolution. Nature 246:96–98 (1973). Original paper.

Ohta extended neutral theory by arguing that some mutations are mildly harmful yet can still spread through random genetic drift. Whether selection efficiently removes them depends on effective population size and the magnitude of their fitness effects. A mutation that is consistently eliminated in a large population may behave almost neutrally in a small one. Her 1973 paper assembled molecular observations and population-genetic arguments suggesting that slightly deleterious substitutions could be especially important in small populations and around speciation. The resulting nearly neutral framework remains central to interpreting molecular evolution, mutation accumulation and differences in the efficacy of selection across lineages.

Beyond biology · A result from outside biology

A huge gas reservoir has almost no visible stars

Monaci M et al. Monthly Notices of the Royal Astronomical Society (online 1 Oct 2026). Original paper.

Two independent radio surveys, conducted with the Arecibo and FAST telescopes, detected neutral hydrogen associated with the same source, AGC 322753. Their signals have similar velocities and double-peaked profiles, consistent with rotating gas. The inferred hydrogen mass is around two billion solar masses, but deep optical images show no clear stellar counterpart. Artificial-galaxy tests suggest that any associated stars could have a mass below roughly fifty million solar masses. This makes the object an intriguing dark-galaxy candidate, not proof of a starless galaxy. The research was published October 1 and reported anew October 26. Deeper optical imaging and sharper radio maps are needed.

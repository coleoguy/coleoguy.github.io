The Tau database.

The free-running circadian period, tau, is the period of an organism's biological clock measured without external time cues. Under constant darkness or constant light, rhythms persist at a period that reflects the endogenous pacemaker. In most organisms tau is close to 24 hours but not exactly, and it varies across taxa, life history stages, and genetic backgrounds. This database compiles free-running period measurements from the primary literature across bacteria, fungi, plants, protists, and animals. Each record includes the mean tau, experimental light condition, species, genotype, and source citation.

What the database holds

Record fields Mean tau, species, genotype, light condition, citation Bacteria, fungi, plants, protists, animals.

Every row in the database is a single free-running period measurement pulled from the primary circadian literature. The record includes the organism at the finest resolution reported (species, strain, genotype), the experimental condition the measurement was taken under (typically constant darkness or constant light), the mean tau, and a citation to the source paper. Records are curated rather than scraped, so downstream users can trust the light condition field in particular, where the literature is inconsistent about reporting.

Interactive views Plots and data table, both filtered live Distribution, by class, by light condition, by order.

The Plots tab shows the tau distribution across all records and broken out by taxonomic class, light condition, and the top 20 orders by record count. A custom plot builder lets you pick axes and colorings to ask your own questions. The Data tab is a sortable, searchable table filtered by class, order, light condition, and sex.

Why it exists

Comparative circadian biology Tau is the basic parameter of the circadian clock Scattered reporting is the main obstacle to comparative work.

The free-running period is the single most-reported quantity in circadian biology, yet there is no central place to retrieve it across species. We built this database because the alternative was for every comparative study to repeat the same literature search from scratch. By curating tau at the tree-of-life scale we make it possible to ask questions that cut across kingdoms: how much does tau vary, how much of that variance is phylogenetic, and what ecological variables actually predict it.

# Chromosome number evolution

## Current understanding

Chromosome number is one of the most dynamic features of eukaryotic genomes, varying enormously even within single orders. In beetles (Coleoptera), which offer the most extensively documented karyotypic record among insects, this variation spans more than an order of magnitude. A database of 4,797 beetle karyotypes reveals that diploid numbers range from as few as 4 chromosomes in the elaterid *Chalcolepidius zonatus* to as many as 70 in the adephagan *Dixus capito obscuroides* [2014b, Finding 1](/knowledge/papers/2014b/#finding-1). This ~17-fold span within a single insect order underscores that chromosome number is far from a fixed species trait — fusions, fissions, and polyploidization can each reshape karyotypes repeatedly over macroevolutionary time.

Beyond simply cataloguing range, a key question is whether chromosome number changes are directional and whether they interact with major transitions in reproductive systems. Evidence from mites (Acari) suggests a striking link: haplodiploid species have significantly lower chromosome numbers than their diplodiploid relatives — approximately 2n = 5 fewer chromosomes on average — and this association holds under both taxonomic and phylogenetic models [2015 haplo, Finding 1](/knowledge/papers/2015_haplo/#finding-1). Ancestral-state reconstructions indicate that karyotype reduction preceded the origin of haplodiploidy rather than following it: across an estimated 7.9–12.9 independent origins of haplodiploidy in Acari, the mean diploid number at origin nodes was 18.4, significantly below the null expectation of 20.2 [2015 haplo, Finding 2](/knowledge/papers/2015_haplo/#finding-2). This temporal ordering is consistent with Bull's haploid-viability hypothesis. The repeated, largely irreversible nature of haplodiploidy origins further suggests that once chromosome number is reduced and haplodiploidy established, the system becomes evolutionarily stable [2015 haplo, Finding 3](/knowledge/papers/2015_haplo/#finding-3).

Reproductive mode also shapes which *type* of karyotypic change predominates. In stick insects (Phasmatodea), rates of polyploidy are significantly elevated in asexually reproducing lineages relative to sexually reproducing ones, while rates of chromosomal fusion and fission do not differ between reproductive modes [2020 polyneoptera, Finding 1](/knowledge/papers/2020_polyneoptera/#finding-1). This suggests that sex acts as a specific brake on polyploidy — perhaps because polyploids face meiotic incompatibilities when crossing with diploids — rather than suppressing structural rearrangements wholesale. Together with the Acari data, these results point toward reproductive system transitions as an important axis along which chromosome number evolution is channelled.

In mammals, a BiSSE analysis comparing mammals with matched versus mismatched karyotypes finds no detectable difference in net diversification rates between these states [2019-mammals, Finding 1](/knowledge/papers/2019_mammals/#finding-1), providing a notable negative result against the chromosomal speciation hypothesis at macroevolutionary scales. The same study also reveals that the rate at which meiotic drive polarity switches varies dramatically across mammalian subclades: Cetartiodactyla show the highest switching rate, with a mean waiting time of approximately 10.8 million years per transition, while Primates show the lowest, with a median waiting time nearly an order of magnitude longer at ~90.9 million years [2019-mammals, Finding 2](/knowledge/papers/2019_mammals/#finding-2).

One might intuitively expect that organisms with more chromosomes — and thus more centromeres and telomeres — would accumulate more microsatellite sequence. Across insects, however, diploid chromosome number shows no significant relationship with either microsatellite content or rate of microsatellite evolution [2020 microsats, Finding 1](/knowledge/papers/2020_microsats/#finding-1). This negative result decouples karyotypic complexity from a major class of repetitive-element dynamics, at least at the whole-genome scale.

## Supporting evidence

The empirical bounds of karyotype variation in Coleoptera — 2n = 4 to 2n = 70 — are drawn from the largest compiled beetle karyotype database to date, encompassing 4,797 karyotypes across the order [2014b, Finding 1](/knowledge/papers/2014b/#finding-1).

In mites, both a taxonomic and a phylogenetic mixed model confirm that haplodiploid species carry approximately 2n = 5 fewer chromosomes than diplodiploid species (P < 0.001) [2015 haplo, Finding 1](/knowledge/papers/2015_haplo/#finding-1). Stochastic character mapping places haplodiploidy origins at nodes with a mean diploid number of 18.4, significantly lower than the null expectation of 20.2 (P = 0.017) [2015 haplo, Finding 2](/knowledge/papers/2015_haplo/#finding-2), with only limited evidence for reversions to diplodiploidy [2015 haplo, Finding 3](/knowledge/papers/2015_haplo/#finding-3).

In Phasmatodea, comparative phylogenetic analysis shows that polyploidy rates are significantly higher in asexual lineages while fusion and fission rates are indistinguishable between sexual and asexual taxa [2020 polyneoptera, Finding 1](/knowledge/papers/2020_polyneoptera/#finding-1).

For mammals, BiSSE analyses across a broad phylogeny detect no difference in speciation or extinction rates tied to karyotype morphology [2019-mammals, Finding 1](/knowledge/papers/2019_mammals/#finding-1). Subclade chromePlus analyses quantify meiotic drive polarity switching at a mean of ~10.8 million years per transition in Cetartiodactyla and a median of ~90.9 million years in Primates [2019-mammals, Finding 2](/knowledge/papers/2019_mammals/#finding-2).

Across a broader insect phylogeny, phylogenetic comparative analyses find no significant association between diploid chromosome number and either microsatellite content or microsatellite evolutionary rate [2020 microsats, Finding 1](/knowledge/papers/2020_microsats/#finding-1), suggesting that karyotype complexity does not predict genome-wide repetitive-element accumulation.

## Contradictions / open disagreements

**BiSSE reliability for the mammalian null result.** The finding that karyotype morphology has no detectable effect on mammalian diversification rates [2019-mammals, Finding 1](/knowledge/papers/2019_mammals/#finding-1) rests on BiSSE, a method known to produce elevated false-positive — and potentially false-negative — rates when diversification is heterogeneous across the tree. The authors themselves document elevated false-positive rates for diversification inference on the cetacean tree in their simulations, so the null result may reflect limited statistical power rather than a true absence of karyotype-driven diversification.

**Mite-specificity of the haplodiploidy–karyotype link.** The 2015 haplo study is restricted to Acari. The authors note that other invertebrate clades with low chromosome numbers have not evolved haplodiploidy, limiting the generality of the chromosome-reduction-first story. Whether the ~2-chromosome difference at haplodiploidy origin nodes constitutes a strong causal signal or a modest, possibly confounded correlation remains open.

**Phasmatodea sampling for asexual polyploidy.** The elevated polyploidy rate in asexual lineages [2020 polyneoptera, Finding 1](/knowledge/papers/2020_polyneoptera/#finding-1) is based on only 13 parthenogenetic species within a single order. The inference also depends on ancestral reconstructions of reproductive mode, which themselves carry uncertainty. Whether this pattern generalises to other asexual arthropods or plants — where polyploidy is common — or whether Phasmatodea represent an idiosyncratic case, remains to be tested with broader sampling.

**Upper bound for Coleoptera.** The value of 2n = 70 for *Dixus capito obscuroides* is drawn from a large database whose verbatim supporting quote is truncated; independent verification against primary sources is advisable before citing this figure downstream [2014b, Finding 1](/knowledge/papers/2014b/#finding-1).

**Meiotic drive switching rates in Primates.** The reported ~90.9 million year figure for Primates is a median waiting time (not a mean), and the inferred rates rely on trees representing only 12–30% of extant species per clade, limiting precision [2019-mammals, Finding 2](/knowledge/papers/2019_mammals/#finding-2).

**Chromosome number and microsatellite content — assembly-coverage caveat.** The null result for chromosome number vs. microsatellite content [2020 microsats, Finding 1](/knowledge/papers/2020_microsats/#finding-1) relies on whole-genome assemblies that typically underrepresent centromeric and telomeric heterochromatin — precisely the regions expected to be enriched near chromosome structural features. The authors acknowledge this limitation explicitly, so a real association concentrated in heterochromatic compartments could be masked by the data.

## Tealc's citation-neighborhood suggestions

- Studies applying ancestral-state reconstruction (e.g., ChromEvol or the Blackmon lab's own approaches) would complement the empirical range data with rate estimates and direction of change across broader insect and mammalian phylogenies.
- Comparative work on holocentric chromosomes in Lepidoptera or Hemiptera could contextualize whether extremely low diploid numbers in some Coleoptera involve mechanistically similar fission/fusion dynamics.
- Tests of Bull's haploid-viability hypothesis in non-Acari haplodiploid clades (Hymenoptera, some scale insects) would help establish whether the chromosome-reduction-first pattern generalizes.
- Hidden-state diversification models (e.g., HiSSE) applied to the mammalian karyotype dataset could address the BiSSE power concerns and provide a more robust test of the chromosomal speciation hypothesis.
- Long-read assembly studies that adequately resolve centromeric and telomeric repeats would provide a more rigorous test of whether chromosome number predicts localized microsatellite enrichment.
- Broader sampling of asexual arthropod and plant lineages would help determine whether the elevated polyploidy rate in asexual Phasmatodea reflects a general consequence of parthenogenesis or an order-specific pattern.

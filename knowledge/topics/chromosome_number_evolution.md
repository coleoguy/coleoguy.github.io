# Chromosome number evolution

## Current understanding

Chromosome number is one of the most dynamic features of eukaryotic genomes, varying enormously even within single orders. In beetles (Coleoptera), which offer the most extensively documented karyotypic record among insects, this variation spans more than an order of magnitude. A database of 4,797 beetle karyotypes reveals that diploid numbers range from as few as 4 chromosomes in the elaterid *Chalcolepidius zonatus* to as many as 70 in the adephagan *Dixus capito obscuroides* [2014b, Finding 1](/knowledge/papers/2014b/#finding-1). This ~17-fold span within a single insect order underscores that chromosome number is far from a fixed species trait — fusions, fissions, and polyploidization can each reshape karyotypes repeatedly over macroevolutionary time.

Beyond simply cataloguing range, a key question is whether chromosome number changes are directional and whether they interact with major transitions in reproductive systems. Evidence from mites (Acari) suggests a striking link: haplodiploid species have significantly lower chromosome numbers than their diplodiploid relatives — approximately 2n = 5 fewer chromosomes on average — and this association holds under both taxonomic and phylogenetic models [2015 haplo, Finding 1](/knowledge/papers/2015_haplo/#finding-1). Critically, ancestral-state reconstructions indicate that karyotype reduction preceded the origin of haplodiploidy rather than following it: across an estimated 7.9–12.9 independent origins of haplodiploidy in Acari, the mean diploid number at origin nodes was 18.4, significantly below the null expectation of 20.2 [2015 haplo, Finding 2](/knowledge/papers/2015_haplo/#finding-2). This temporal ordering is consistent with Bull's haploid-viability hypothesis, which posits that a reduced karyotype facilitates the transition to haplodiploidy by lowering the fitness costs of haploid expression in males.

The repeated, largely irreversible nature of haplodiploidy origins in mites — with limited statistical support for reversions back to diplodiploidy — further suggests that once chromosome number is reduced and haplodiploidy established, the system is evolutionarily stable [2015 haplo, Finding 3](/knowledge/papers/2015_haplo/#finding-3).

## Supporting evidence

The empirical bounds of karyotype variation in Coleoptera — 2n = 4 to 2n = 70 — are drawn from the largest compiled beetle karyotype database to date, encompassing 4,797 karyotypes across the order [2014b, Finding 1](/knowledge/papers/2014b/#finding-1).

In mites, both a taxonomic and a phylogenetic mixed model confirm that haplodiploid species carry approximately 2n = 5 fewer chromosomes than diplodiploid species (P < 0.001) [2015 haplo, Finding 1](/knowledge/papers/2015_haplo/#finding-1). Stochastic character mapping on a mite phylogeny places haplodiploidy origins at nodes with a mean diploid number of 18.4, significantly lower than the null expectation of 20.2 (P = 0.017), supporting the idea that low chromosome number is an antecedent condition rather than a consequence of haplodiploidy [2015 haplo, Finding 2](/knowledge/papers/2015_haplo/#finding-2). Haplodiploidy itself has arisen between 7.9 and 12.9 times in Acari depending on whether reversions are modeled, with only limited evidence that transitions back to diplodiploidy occur [2015 haplo, Finding 3](/knowledge/papers/2015_haplo/#finding-3).

## Contradictions / open disagreements

**Mite-specificity of the haplodiploidy–karyotype link.** The 2015 haplo study is restricted to Acari. The authors note that other invertebrate clades with low chromosome numbers and male heterogamety have not evolved haplodiploidy, limiting the generality of the chromosome-reduction-first story. Whether the ~2-chromosome difference in mean diploid number at haplodiploidy origin nodes constitutes a strong causal signal or a modest, possibly confounded correlation remains debated; the authors themselves flag that phylogenies provide limited resolution on the timing of co-evolutionary events.

**Upper bound for Coleoptera.** The value of 2n = 70 for *Dixus capito obscuroides* is drawn from a large database entry whose verbatim supporting quote is truncated; independent verification against the primary source is advisable before citing this figure downstream [2014b, Finding 1](/knowledge/papers/2014b/#finding-1).

## Tealc's citation-neighborhood suggestions

- Studies applying ancestral-state reconstruction (e.g., ChromEvol or the Blackmon lab's own approaches) would complement the empirical range data with rate estimates and direction of change across broader insect phylogenies.
- Comparative work on holocentric chromosomes in Lepidoptera or Hemiptera could contextualize whether the extremely low diploid numbers in some Coleoptera are mechanistically similar to fission/fusion dynamics in other orders.
- Tests of Bull's haploid-viability hypothesis in non-Acari haplodiploid clades (Hymenoptera, some scale insects) would help establish whether the chromosome-reduction-first pattern generalizes.

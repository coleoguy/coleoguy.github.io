# Spinal Cord Injury

## Current understanding

Neural progenitor cell (NPC) grafts have emerged as a promising strategy for rebuilding circuitry after spinal cord injury (SCI), but a critical open question is *how* graft-derived neurons actually connect to the host nervous system. Recent work using complementary viral circuit-tracing tools — monosynaptic rabies virus and polysynaptic pseudorabies virus (PRV) — has begun to place quantitative numbers on this connectivity.

The picture that emerges is one of sparse direct contact but richer indirect integration. Monosynaptic connections between graft-derived neurons and host lumbar motor neurons are measurable but low: a rabies infectivity index of approximately 1.59 (normalized rabies-positive graft neurons per primarily infected motor neuron starter cell) indicates that direct, single-synapse linkages exist, but are not the dominant mode of circuit re-engagement ([2025 circuitry injured spine, Finding 1](/knowledge/papers/2025_circuitry_injured_spine/#finding-1)).

By contrast, polysynaptic connectivity — chains of two or more synapses linking graft neurons into hindlimb motor circuits — is roughly four times more prevalent. The PRV infectivity index of ~6.39 suggests that graft-derived neurons are woven into the broader motor circuitry primarily through relay pathways rather than through direct projections onto motor neurons ([2025 circuitry injured spine, Finding 2](/knowledge/papers/2025_circuitry_injured_spine/#finding-2)). This asymmetry matters for therapeutic interpretation: functional recovery supported by NPC grafts may depend more on reconstructing intermediate interneuronal relays than on re-establishing direct corticospinal or propriospinal-to-motor-neuron synapses.

These findings shift the mechanistic framing of NPC-based SCI therapy. Rather than expecting grafts to act as simple "bridges" that directly reconnect descending axons to motor neurons, the data point toward a model in which grafts serve as polysynaptic hubs embedded within a reorganized lumbar motor network.

## Supporting evidence

- Monosynaptic graft-to-host connectivity (rabies infectivity index ≈ 1.59 ± 0.55) provides a quantitative floor for direct circuit integration in a mouse contusion model: [2025 circuitry injured spine, Finding 1](/knowledge/papers/2025_circuitry_injured_spine/#finding-1)

- Polysynaptic connectivity (PRV infectivity index ≈ 6.39 ± 1.43) is approximately four times greater than monosynaptic connectivity, indicating that graft neurons participate mainly through multi-step relays: [2025 circuitry injured spine, Finding 2](/knowledge/papers/2025_circuitry_injured_spine/#finding-2)

## Contradictions / open disagreements

No direct contradictions exist within the current set of findings; both derive from the same experimental system. However, several methodological tensions deserve attention:

- **Rabies tracing** relies on helper virus expression in starter cells and may underestimate connectivity if helper virus transduction is incomplete or variable across motor neurons.
- **PRV spread** is sensitive to replication kinetics and the chosen survival endpoint (72 hours); a longer or shorter window could substantially alter the apparent polysynaptic index, making cross-study comparisons difficult.
- Whether these connectivity indices predict or correlate with *functional* motor recovery remains to be established — circuit anatomy and behavioral outcome are not equivalent.

Broader replication across injury models (e.g., complete transection vs. contusion), species, graft cell types, and post-injury timepoints is needed before these indices can serve as universal benchmarks.

## Tealc's citation-neighborhood suggestions

- Studies characterizing the electrophysiological properties of graft-derived synapses (e.g., EPSPs recorded from identified motor neurons post-grafting) would complement the anatomical infectivity indices with functional validation.
- Work on interneuronal relay circuits in uninjured spinal cord (e.g., propriospinal neuron connectivity maps) would provide a natural baseline against which to compare graft-derived polysynaptic indices.
- Comparative NPC graft studies in non-human primates or larger contusion models would test generalizability of the mouse findings.

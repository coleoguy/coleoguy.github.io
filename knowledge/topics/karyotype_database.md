# Karyotype Database

## Current understanding

Comprehensive karyotype databases aggregate cytogenetic records across the animal tree of life, enabling broad comparative analyses of sex determination and chromosome evolution. Two notable resources illustrate both the power and the limits of this approach.

For insects, the Tree of Sex database underpins the finding that male heterogamety (XY or XO systems) is the dominant sex-determination mode, documented in 24 of 28 insect orders and encompassing approximately 77% of sexually reproducing insect species surveyed ([2015b, Finding 1](/knowledge/papers/2015b/#finding-1)). This quantitative baseline is crucial for evaluating hypotheses about ancestral states, transition rates between sex-determination systems, and the evolutionary forces that maintain or erode sex chromosomes across lineages.

For amphibians, a dedicated Amphibian Karyotype Database now contains 2124 records — more than double the records in the previous compilation published nearly 30 years earlier. The vast majority of records (1833) cover frogs (Anura), while salamanders (Caudata) and caecilians (Gymnophiona) contribute only 246 and 45 records, respectively ([2019 amphibs, Finding 1](/knowledge/papers/2019_amphibs/#finding-1)). This two-fold expansion since 1990 makes the resource a substantially more useful foundation for macroevolutionary inference across tetrapods, even as it highlights how unevenly cytogenetic effort has been distributed across amphibian orders.

Both databases share a structural challenge: their scope reflects the cumulative effort of decades of karyotyping work and therefore skews toward historically well-studied groups. Well-characterized lineages contribute disproportionately to current counts, while smaller or cryptic orders remain undersampled. Nonetheless, the scale of existing records — spanning thousands of species across multiple animal phyla — makes curated karyotype databases an indispensable scaffold for comparative biology.

## Supporting evidence

- [2015b, Finding 1](/knowledge/papers/2015b/#finding-1): Male heterogamety (XY/XO) has been documented in 24 of 28 insect orders and encompasses 77% of sexually reproducing insect species investigated, establishing it as the most common sex-determination mode across insects.
- [2019 amphibs, Finding 1](/knowledge/papers/2019_amphibs/#finding-1): The Amphibian Karyotype Database contains 2124 karyotype records — more than doubling the 1990 compilation — with 1833 Anura, 246 Caudata, and 45 Gymnophiona records, providing the most comprehensive amphibian cytogenetic resource to date.

## Contradictions / open disagreements

The 77% insect male-heterogamety figure rests on a taxonomically uneven sampling frame; orders that are species-rich but poorly studied cytogenetically are underrepresented, and future database expansions could revise this proportion in either direction.

Similarly, the Amphibian Karyotype Database's 2124 records are heavily skewed toward frogs, with caecilians in particular remaining dramatically undersampled (45 records). Analyses drawing on either database must account for uneven taxonomic representation, and prevalence or transition-rate estimates derived from them carry an inherent ascertainment-bias caveat. No direct contradicting paper is currently cited, but the sampling imbalance is a standing limitation for any macroevolutionary inference built on these resources.

## Tealc's citation-neighborhood suggestions

- White (1973) *Animal Cytology and Evolution* — the foundational text for insect karyotype cataloguing against which database efforts are benchmarked.
- Pennell et al. (2015) *Tree of Sex: A comprehensive database of sexual reproduction* — the primary database paper underlying the insect statistics cited here.
- Bachtrog et al. (2014) *Sex determination: Why so many ways of doing it?* — broad comparative framework situating database findings within theory.
- Bogart (1980) / Duellman & Trueb (1986) — earlier amphibian karyotype compilations against which the 2019 database expansion is benchmarked.

# Karyotype Database

## Current understanding

Comprehensive karyotype databases aggregate cytogenetic records across the animal tree of life, enabling broad comparative analyses of sex determination and chromosome evolution. Three complementary resources illustrate both the power and the limits of this approach.

At the broadest scale, a synthesis across 10,754 surveyed plant and animal species (excluding 1,453 species with multiple sex chromosomes likely arising from sex chromosome–autosome fusions) finds that 67% carry XX/XY systems and roughly 28% carry XX/XO systems, with only a single known species exhibiting a YO/WO univalent sex-specific chromosome ([2022 why not, Finding 1](/knowledge/papers/2022_why_not/#finding-1)). This eukaryote-wide baseline establishes XX/XY as the dominant configuration by a wide margin and provides a quantitative scaffold for evaluating how particular lineages deviate from the norm.

For insects specifically, the Tree of Sex database underpins the finding that male heterogamety (XY or XO systems) is documented in 24 of 28 insect orders and encompasses approximately 77% of sexually reproducing insect species surveyed ([2015b, Finding 1](/knowledge/papers/2015b/#finding-1)). This figure is consistent with the broader eukaryote pattern and is crucial for evaluating hypotheses about ancestral states, transition rates between sex-determination systems, and the evolutionary forces that maintain or erode sex chromosomes.

For amphibians, a dedicated Amphibian Karyotype Database contains 2,124 records — more than double the records in the previous compilation published nearly 30 years earlier. The vast majority of records (1,833) cover frogs (Anura), while salamanders (Caudata) and caecilians (Gymnophiona) contribute only 246 and 45 records, respectively ([2019 amphibs, Finding 1](/knowledge/papers/2019_amphibs/#finding-1)). This two-fold expansion makes the resource a substantially more useful foundation for macroevolutionary inference across tetrapods, even as it highlights how unevenly cytogenetic effort has been distributed across amphibian orders.

All three resources share a structural challenge: their scope reflects the cumulative effort of decades of karyotyping work and therefore skews toward historically well-studied groups and historically favored detection methods. In particular, female-heterogamety (ZW) systems are almost certainly underestimated in current databases because many classical karyotyping protocols are less sensitive to heteromorphic W chromosomes, meaning the reported prevalence figures reflect sampling effort as much as biological reality.

## Supporting evidence

- [2022 why not, Finding 1](/knowledge/papers/2022_why_not/#finding-1): Among 10,754 surveyed species (excluding multi-sex-chromosome systems), 67% exhibit XX/XY and ~28% exhibit XX/XO sex chromosome systems, establishing XX/XY as the most common configuration across eukaryotes.
- [2015b, Finding 1](/knowledge/papers/2015b/#finding-1): Male heterogamety (XY/XO) has been documented in 24 of 28 insect orders and encompasses 77% of sexually reproducing insect species investigated, establishing it as the most common sex-determination mode across insects.
- [2019 amphibs, Finding 1](/knowledge/papers/2019_amphibs/#finding-1): The Amphibian Karyotype Database contains 2,124 karyotype records — more than doubling the 1990 compilation — with 1,833 Anura, 246 Caudata, and 45 Gymnophiona records, providing the most comprehensive amphibian cytogenetic resource to date.

## Contradictions / open disagreements

The cross-eukaryote 67%/28% split ([2022 why not, Finding 1](/knowledge/papers/2022_why_not/#finding-1)) and the 77% insect male-heterogamety figure ([2015b, Finding 1](/knowledge/papers/2015b/#finding-1)) are broadly consistent, but both rest on taxonomically uneven sampling frames. The 2022 synthesis explicitly acknowledges that female heterogamety is "undoubtedly an under-estimate," meaning ZW prevalence could be substantially higher than current records suggest. The insect figure similarly reflects the cytogenetic effort invested in well-studied orders; species-rich but poorly karyotyped lineages are underrepresented, and future expansions could shift the proportion in either direction.

The Amphibian Karyotype Database's 2,124 records are heavily skewed toward frogs, with caecilians in particular remaining dramatically undersampled (45 records). Any macroevolutionary inference built on these databases must account for ascertainment bias — both taxonomic (which lineages were studied) and methodological (which sex-chromosome morphologies are detectable by classical cytogenetics).

## Tealc's citation-neighborhood suggestions

- White (1973) *Animal Cytology and Evolution* — the foundational text for insect karyotype cataloguing against which database efforts are benchmarked.
- Pennell et al. (2015) *Tree of Sex: A comprehensive database of sexual reproduction* — the primary database paper underlying the insect statistics cited here.
- Bachtrog et al. (2014) *Sex determination: Why so many ways of doing it?* — broad comparative framework situating database findings within theory.
- Bogart (1980) / Duellman & Trueb (1986) — earlier amphibian karyotype compilations against which the 2019 database expansion is benchmarked.

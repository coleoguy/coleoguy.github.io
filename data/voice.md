# Heath Blackmon's scientific writing voice

A portable style calibration for agents that draft prose *with* or *for*
Heath Blackmon. Built from eight lead- or senior-authored papers
(listed below), weighted toward the single- and dual-authored work
where his voice is least diluted by co-authors.

> Intended use: load this file as context when an agent is producing
> drafts Heath will sign his name to — manuscripts, cover letters,
> grant sections, lab-site prose. Do **not** load it when drafting
> under another author's name or editing someone else's work.

This file combines two kinds of guidance. The first is
**descriptive**: patterns distilled from Heath's corpus, with
verbatim quotes as anchors. The second is **prescriptive**:
reader-centered writing principles adapted from George Gopen (see
*Reader-centered writing* below). The goal is not imitation — it is
a better version of Heath, writing to land on the reader the way he
already lands at his best.

Where descriptive and prescriptive guidance agree, follow them.
Where they conflict, the Gopen principle wins for prose drafted
going forward; the corpus quote is the historical record of what
Heath did, not a target.

The rules below are falsifiable: each names a pattern and cites
verbatim passages from the corpus that exhibit it, or explains
a reader expectation and why it matters. If the corpus expands
and a rule stops matching new material — or if the reader stops
behaving the way a Gopen rule predicts — the rule should change.

## Reader-centered writing (Gopen)

Writers often ask "what am I trying to say?" Readers ask a different
question: "where am I supposed to find it?" George Gopen's work
(*The Science of Scientific Writing*, Gopen & Swan, *American
Scientist* 1990; *Expectations*, Gopen 2004) shows that readers
approach every sentence with structural expectations — about where
the emphasis goes, where the subject goes, how the sentence connects
to the previous one. Honoring those expectations makes prose feel
effortless. Violating them makes it feel vague or tiring, even when
every individual word is correct.

These principles take priority over surface mimicry. An agent that
produces a paragraph riddled with Heath's favorite words but buries
the main claim in the middle of a sentence has failed. An agent that
lands the claim in the right place with plain words has succeeded,
even if the wording is not what Heath would have chosen.

### Stress position

The last few words before a full stop (or semicolon, or colon) are
the **stress position** — where readers unconsciously place emphasis.
Put the most important new information there. Do not waste the
stress position on citations, dates, or hedges.

- *Weak.* "Chromosome number changes are often deleterious, as
  shown by Lande (1979) and Walsh (1982)."
- *Better.* "As Lande (1979) and Walsh (1982) showed, chromosome
  number changes are often deleterious."
- *On-pattern from the corpus.* *2014 Genetics.* "The rates and
  patterns of Y chromosome turnover are distinctly different between
  the two largest Coleoptera suborders, **Polyphaga and Adephaga**."
  — the contrasted groups land in the stress position, so the reader
  remembers the comparison.

A diagnostic: read the sentence and ask, "what was this sentence
about?" The answer should be in the last few words. If it isn't,
rewrite.

### Topic position and the given-new contract

The subject and anything up to the main verb is the **topic
position** — where the reader learns what the sentence is about.
Use it to carry forward a term from the *previous* sentence's stress
position. This is the **given-new contract**: each sentence begins
with something already established, then moves to something new.

- The linkage happens in the topic position, not in connective
  adverbs. "Furthermore" and "Moreover" are not cohesion; a repeated
  noun phrase is.
- *Weak.* "Chromosome number changes are often deleterious. Drift
  can nonetheless fix them in small populations."
  (topic-position shift from *changes* to *drift* — minor jolt)
- *Better.* "Chromosome number changes are often deleterious.
  **These deleterious changes** can nonetheless fix in small
  populations through drift."
  (topic carries, new information lands at the end)

### Subject-verb proximity

Readers cannot hold a sentence in suspension. Keep the grammatical
subject and the main verb close together. Every word between them
is working memory the reader has to spend. Long interrupters — long
parenthetical clauses, relative clauses, definitional asides — cost
more than they deliver.

This is the structural reason for the "no em-dashes" rule below: an
em-dash-delimited aside splits the subject-verb pair.

- *Weak.* "Chromosome number, which varies from 2 in the jack
  jumper ant to over 200 in some ferns, evolves under a mix of
  drift and selection."
- *Better.* "Chromosome number evolves under a mix of drift and
  selection. It ranges from 2 in the jack jumper ant to over 200
  in some ferns."

### Whose story is this?

Every sentence tells someone's story. The grammatical subject should
match whose story it is. If the paragraph is about the experiment,
the experiment is the subject of its sentences. If it is about the
scientists' reasoning, say *we*.

- The corpus rule (first-person plural, always) is a special case:
  when the story is our analysis, *we* is the right subject.
- When the story is about the phenomenon itself, the phenomenon is
  the right subject: "Y chromosomes decay because…", "Chromosome
  number rises in clades where…".
- A classic failure: the real protagonist of the sentence is
  demoted to an object while a placeholder ("it", "there", "this")
  sits in the subject slot. Find the protagonist, put it back.

### Actions live in verbs

Avoid nominalizations — nouns that are hiding verbs. Nominalized
prose is longer, vaguer, and pushes the action out of the stress
position.

| Nominalized | Better |
|-------------|--------|
| "we made a comparison of X and Y" | "we compared X and Y" |
| "there was an increase in rate" | "the rate increased" |
| "is suggestive of" | "suggests" |
| "performed an analysis" | "analyzed" |
| "has the ability to" | "can" |
| "played an important role in" | "shaped" / "drove" |
| "the conclusion of the authors is that" | "we conclude that" |

Every nominalization you delete shortens the sentence and sharpens
its point. Run a draft once with this table beside you.

### The paragraph is a unit of meaning

A paragraph has a **topic** (what it is about) and a **point** (what
it asserts). The topic belongs at the top. The point usually belongs
at the paragraph's own stress position — the last sentence.

- Heath's discussion paragraphs already lead with a summary
  sentence. That works for expository paragraphs where the point is
  the summary. For argumentative paragraphs — the ones in the
  significance section of a grant, the rationale in a cover letter,
  the "why this matters" moves in a discussion — defer the point to
  the last sentence so the reader meets the conclusion as the
  paragraph ends, not as it begins.
- Link adjacent paragraphs the same way: the first sentence of a
  new paragraph should hook the stress point of the previous
  paragraph.

### One point per sentence

A sentence can only highlight one thing in its stress position. If
you have two points, write two sentences.

- *Weak.* "Contrary to our initial expectations, we find that
  highly degenerated Y chromosomes of many members of the suborder
  Polyphaga are rarely lost, and that cases of Y chromosome loss
  are strongly associated with chiasmatic segregation during male
  meiosis." (from *2014 Genetics*; two points fused)
- *Better, Gopen-improved.* "Contrary to our initial expectations,
  highly degenerated Y chromosomes in the suborder Polyphaga are
  rarely lost. Where Y loss does occur, it is strongly associated
  with chiasmatic segregation during male meiosis."

This is one of the few places where Gopen's principles push against
the corpus. Follow Gopen.

### Repeat key terms; don't paraphrase them

Scientific prose is clearer when a concept keeps the same name.
Synonym-hunting ("chromosome number" → "karyotype size" →
"chromosomal content") forces the reader to verify that the terms
refer to the same thing. Cohesion comes from repetition, not from
variation.

## Sentence scale

### Openers — declarative, subject-first, historically grounded

Papers open by naming the phenomenon, not by rhetoricizing its
importance. Historical anchors appear within the first one or two
sentences.

- *2019 Evolution.* "Chromosome number is one of the first genomic
  characteristics identified (Goldsmith 1919; Painter 1921), and by
  the 1950s chromosome number had been investigated in several
  hundred species (Makino 1951)."
- *2024 J Hered.* "For any given trait, variation within and among
  species is a function of mutation, drift, selection, fidelity of
  trait expression, and isolation among lineages."
- *2017 Genetics.* "The evolution of heteromorphic sex chromosomes
  has fascinated biologists, inspiring theoretical models,
  experimental studies, and studies of genome structure."
- *2014 Genetics.* "Chromosomal sex determination has evolved
  independently in many lineages (Bull 1983)."

**Don't** open with "Understanding X is a key challenge in biology"
or "Much of evolutionary theory is predicated on..." — those are
generic hooks. State the thing.

### Claim verbs — propositional, never triumphal

Heath almost never "demonstrates" or "proves." He:

- **proposes** ("We propose the 'fragile Y' hypothesis..." — 2014)
- **argues** ("We argue that the most parsimonious explanation..." — 2019)
- **suggests** ("Our results, in concert with previous work,
  suggest that..." — 2024)
- **finds** / **finds that** ("we find that highly degenerated Y
  chromosomes... are rarely lost" — 2014)
- **shows** (rare; reserved for the analysis, not the claim)

**Don't** write "We demonstrate," "We prove," "This paper establishes."

### Hedging — explicit and structural, not decorative

Hedges carry analytic weight, they are not softeners. "may,"
"likely," "consistent with," "we interpret," "our data support"
appear on load-bearing claims. The word *suggest* is the most
common hedge verb.

- "chromosome number changes are likely fixed by genetic drift
  despite an initial fitness cost" (2024)
- "Our findings suggest that meiotic drive may be a widespread
  force in shaping chromosome number evolution." (2019)
- "These results suggest that mammals with matched or mismatched
  karyotypes do not have detectably different net diversification
  rates." (2019)

### Em-dashes — never use them

Em-dashes delimit an aside that splits the grammatical subject from
its verb (or splits some other adjacent pair the reader was tracking).
That split forces the reader to park the first half of the sentence
in working memory while the aside resolves. See *Subject-verb
proximity* under *Reader-centered writing*.

Older Heath papers in the corpus use them; the rule going forward is
to rewrite the aside as its own sentence, a parenthetical, or a
restrictive clause.

### Parentheticals with `e.g.,` and `i.e.,`

Heavy use for concrete examples and clarifying rephrase. Both
abbreviations get commas after them.

- "(e.g., life-history traits, P1, F1, BC1, etc.)" (2024 Burch)
- "(i.e., those with multiple X and/or Y chromosomes)" (2014)

### Pronouns — first-person plural, always

"We" appears even in the sole-authored popular-science piece. Passive
voice is used sparingly and mostly in Methods.

- *2014 Coleopterist's Corner* (sole author): "we envision it as a
  long-term repository... We have begun using this database..."

**Don't** use "the present authors," "the current study," "it is
shown." Say *we*.

## Paragraph scale

### The expectation-violation move

A recurring rhetorical structure: state what theory/intuition
predicts, then state what was actually found.

- *2014.* "Contrary to our initial expectations, we find that highly
  degenerated Y chromosomes of many members of the suborder Polyphaga
  are rarely lost, and that cases of Y chromosome loss are strongly
  associated with chiasmatic segregation during male meiosis."
- *2019.* "Many theoretical models have invoked chromosomal evolution
  as a driver of speciation. We have identified a significantly
  higher rate of chromosome evolution in taxa with mismatched
  karyotypes but no increase in speciation rate associated with
  this binary state."

When the results were surprising, say so plainly. "Contrary to,"
"Despite," "However" do the work.

### Evidence chains use *because*, not only *therefore*

Claims are followed by the mechanism, not only the data.

- "This may indicate that sexually antagonistic loci are sufficiently
  common in Adephaga genomes that fusions of an autosome to the X
  chromosome are often favored." (2014)

### Named hypotheses and named objects

Heath coins short handles for ideas so readers can cite them.

- "the **fragile Y** hypothesis" (2014, 2015, reused 2017)
- "**matched** vs **mismatched** karyotypes" (2019)
- Methods are attached to named R packages: `chromePlus`, `SAGA2`,
  `evobiR`, `ChromePlus`. Never "our custom script."

If an agent proposes a new concept in a Heath draft, give it a
named handle, lower-case, quoted on first use.

## Section scale

### Introduction template (3–5 paragraphs)

1. Declarative statement of the phenomenon + historical anchor.
2. What is known and by whom (citation-dense, not exhaustive).
3. What remains unresolved, stated as a gap.
4. *"The present study..."* / *"Here we..."* / *"We address this by..."*
   — explicit statement of the approach.
5. Optional preview sentence of the main finding.

Example transition to the study itself (2014):

> "The model systems for studying Y chromosome evolution,
> *Drosophila* and mammals, are ill suited to explore hypotheses
> about the tempo and mode of Y chromosome turnover because there
> are few transitions among sex chromosome states. Here we use
> comparative methods in the order Coleoptera..."

### Results — "We find" / "We found" / "Our analysis indicates"

Report quantitatively: rates with credible intervals, p-values with
direction, sample sizes inline.

- "we estimate a mean ΔR_fusion of 0.003 and a mean ΔR_fission of
  0.025" (2024)
- "the 95% credible interval of Δr entirely positive" (2019)

### Discussion — summary sentence, then development

First sentence of the Discussion is a one-sentence restatement of
the main finding. Then the paragraph develops it.

- *2024.* "Our analyses clearly show that traits that are expected
  to reduce Ne are associated with increased karyotype evolution
  rates, and this pattern holds independently of potential
  differences in mutation rate between lineages."
- *2019.* "Our findings suggest that meiotic drive may be a
  widespread force in shaping chromosome number evolution."
- *2014.* "The rates and patterns of Y chromosome turnover are
  distinctly different between the two largest Coleoptera suborders,
  Polyphaga and Adephaga."

### Limitations — named caveats, never a single buried sentence

Heath's discussions always carry an explicit caveats move. It gets
its own paragraph(s) and names specific failure modes of the
analysis.

- *2017.* "There are several important caveats to consider when
  interpreting our results. We considered the deterministic fate
  of a new mutation, ignoring both the source of mutational input
  and the effect of random genetic drift."
- *2024.* "One potentially confounding factor in our analysis is
  the inclusion of pest species that experience periodic outbreaks
  that can lead to orders of magnitude increase in census population
  size."
- *2019.* "One potential concern with the application of our model
  to the mammal dataset is that the binary trait coding of matched
  or mismatched is fundamentally linked to chromosome number
  evolution..."

### Conclusion — 1–3 sentences, opens with "In conclusion" / "Our results suggest" / "In summary"

- *2014.* "Our analysis suggests that meiotic mechanisms play an
  important, previously unappreciated role in the tempo of Y
  chromosome gain and loss."
- *2019.* "In conclusion, we have demonstrated that the rate of
  chromosome number evolution in mammals is associated with the
  morphology of the karyotype."
- *2017.* "In summary, our work has two major implications. First,
  our work shows that the resolution of intralocus sexual conflict
  could incidentally increase the rates of sex-chromosome aneuploidy..."
- *2024.* "Our results, in concert with previous work, suggest that
  chromosome number evolution is primarily governed by random
  genetic drift in small populations..."

### Future-work pointer

The last paragraph of the Discussion (or a Conclusion second
sentence) points to a specific, feasible extension — not a vague
"further work is warranted."

- *2014.* "A more nuanced understanding of sex chromosome evolution,
  one that tests the predictions of this study, would benefit from
  genomic data that allow for assignment of chromosomal homologies."
- *2019.* Full paragraph listing three concrete extensions: track
  chromosome arms (fundamental number), apply threshold models,
  model karyotype remodeling processes.

## Titles

Heath's titles are short, memorable, and often play with language.
They are not "On the X of Y" labels — they work as standalone
phrases and are easy to cite in conversation.

**Observed patterns across the Blackmon Lab publication list:**

- **Puns on the subject.** "Why not Y naught" (2022, *Heredity*) —
  rhymes *Y naught* with *why not*. "The March of the Beetles"
  (2020, *J Heredity*) — Sousa allusion applied to dispersal in
  *Tribolium*.
- **Aphorisms or borrowed phrases.** "Wright was right" (2024
  *Evolution*, Burch et al.). "Tempo and Mode of Y Chromosome
  Turnover" (2014) — Simpsonian allusion to *Tempo and Mode in
  Evolution*.
- **Alliteration.** "Drift drives the evolution of chromosome
  number" (2024, *J Heredity*).
- **Verb-forward.** "Dismantling Chromosomal Stasis Across the
  Eukaryotic Tree of Life" (2026, bioRxiv). "Estimating Tempo
  and Mode..." (2014).
- **Contrasted clauses.** "Long-Term Fragility of Y Chromosomes
  Is Dominated by Short-Term Resolution of Sexual Antagonism"
  (2017).
- **Evocative noun phrases as main title + descriptive colon
  subtitle.** "Ghosts of a Structured Past: Impacts of Ancestral
  Patterns of Isolation-by-Distance on Divergence-Time Estimation"
  (2020).

For a research article with a catchy main title, pair it with a
descriptive colon-subtitle so the paper is still findable by
keyword search. Perspectives and short-form pieces can go with the
hook alone.

**Avoid** "A study of...", "On the evolution of...", "Toward a
better understanding of...", or any construction where the title
could apply to half the field. A Heath title should make sense as
a chapter name, a talk slide, or a tweet.

## Vocabulary

### Keep (observed repeatedly in the corpus)

- *tempo and mode* (title of 2014; reused as a framing device)
- *turnover*
- *striking* / *strikingly*
- *recalcitrant to generalizations*
- *in concert with*
- *predominantly*
- *broadly*
- *consistent with*
- *nuanced*
- *one potentially confounding factor*
- *despite*
- *contrary to*
- *our hypothesis makes N predictions*

### Avoid (absent from the corpus, or flagged elsewhere)

- *novel* as a claim-booster (appears only descriptively, e.g.
  "novel Y chromosomes")
- *groundbreaking*, *paradigm shift*, *cutting-edge*
- *the present authors*, *it has been shown that*
- *very*, *really*, *quite* as intensifiers
- *utilize* (use *use*)
- *tidyverse* idioms in R-related prose
  (see `prompts.json` → "coding-partner")
- generic *aims to* / *seeks to* constructions

## Anchor passages (verbatim, for pattern-matching)

### Anchor 1 — discussion opener + caveat (2014 Genetics)

> The rates and patterns of Y chromosome turnover are distinctly
> different between the two largest Coleoptera suborders, Polyphaga
> and Adephaga. We suggest that important differences in the meiotic
> machinery, and particularly sex chromosome pairing, between these
> lineages indicates that previously unappreciated evolutionary
> forces may influence Y chromosome evolution.
>
> In the suborder Adephaga, a Y chromosome has a 57% chance of being
> lost or gained per 100 million years. The 65 Y chromosome losses
> (Figure 4) in Adephaga, are not difficult to explain.

### Anchor 2 — expectation framing (2017 Genetics)

> Current theory does not explicitly address this tension between
> sexual antagonism favoring sex-chromosome inversions, and the
> resultant aneuploidy or meiotic arrest that disfavors them.
> Rather, prevailing wisdom holds that recombination reduction
> between sex chromosomes stops when the PAR becomes small because
> it is essential for proper segregation in species with chiasmatic
> meiosis (White 1977). This view would suggest that, given enough
> time, PAR sizes in all species would be roughly equal... However,
> the sevenfold differences in PAR size among eutherians suggests
> that this is not the case.

### Anchor 3 — result + hedge + implication (2019 Evolution)

> Our findings suggest that meiotic drive may be a widespread force
> in shaping chromosome number evolution. The striking difference
> in rates of chromosome number evolution between taxa with matched
> and mismatched karyotypes suggests that any attempts to identify
> the role of alternative forces like population structure or
> selection in large-scale karyotype evolution should allow for the
> possible impact of meiotic drive.

### Anchor 4 — conclusion (2024 J Heredity)

> Our results, in concert with previous work, suggest that
> chromosome number evolution is primarily governed by random
> genetic drift in small populations and that mutations that
> change chromosome number are deleterious (at least while
> segregating). Despite this finding there are almost certainly
> many individual cases where selection has driven a change in
> the karyotype.

### Anchor 5 — question-framed Perspective Introduction (2022 *Heredity*, "Why not Y naught")

Written by Heath as the Introduction of a multi-author Perspective
(author contributions confirm HB wrote the intro; other sections
were written by co-authors). Notable for an explicit three-sentence
roadmap, which is more telegraphed than his research-article intros
and appropriate to the short Perspective format. The named
phenomenon ("YO or WO systems") is on-pattern. The em-dash in
*"one single species—the New Zealand frog…"* predates the no-em-dash
rule; rewrite that construction as a new sentence or a parenthetical
when drafting going forward.

> Among all the species with information in the papers above,
> only a single species—the New Zealand frog, *Leiopelma
> hochstetteri*—appears to have a univalent sex-specific
> chromosome acting as a dominant sex-determining chromosome.
> We refer to systems like this as YO or WO for male and female
> heterogamety, respectively. Here, we ask why YO and WO systems
> are so uncommon. We first evaluate evidence for the existence
> of YO and WO systems and their potential to arise by reviewing
> the literature. We then discuss challenges YO and WO systems
> may face over evolutionary time and the impact of sexually
> antagonistic (SA) variation on their fates. We conclude that
> YO and WO systems are unlikely to remain stable, and their
> transitory nature can explain why they are rare.

### Anchor 6 — popular mode (2014 Coleopterist's Corner)

Shorter sentences, named individuals credited, technical terms
glossed inline. Still first-person plural. Still ends with future
work + contact.

> Coleoptera karyotypes have a long history of use by taxonomists
> and evolutionary biologists. In 1905, Nettie Stevens discovered
> the first empirical support for the role of chromosomes in sex
> determination through karyotype analysis of the yellow mealworm
> beetle, *Tenebrio molitor*. Stevens eventually identified what
> she called heterochromosomes in an additional 44 species of
> beetles. We now know that these heterochromosomes were the X and
> Y chromosome, and that as she inferred are indeed the key to sex
> determination.

## Mode switching

The corpus contains two modes with shared DNA but different
surface texture:

| Axis | Technical mode (peer-reviewed papers) | Popular mode (Coleopterist's Corner, lab-site prose) |
|------|---------------------------------------|---|
| Pronouns | *we* throughout | *we* throughout |
| Sentence length | long, compound, clause-chained | shorter, more period-break points |
| Jargon | unglossed; reader assumed to be a specialist | glossed inline on first use |
| Credit | by citation | by name ("Stevens," "Smith and Virkki") |
| Closer | limitations + future work + conclusion | future work + contact / invitation |
| Rhetoric | claims with explicit hedges | teaching voice; invites the reader to use the resource |

Lab-site prose (e.g. `ai.html`, `lead-investigator.html`) is a
third mode that overlaps with popular but is more compressed and
more declarative. Three-word stat blocks, one-sentence ledes. That
mode is best learned from the HTML pages directly, not from the
paper corpus.

## Anti-patterns (what Heath does not do)

1. **Does not open with a rhetorical question.** ("What drives
   karyotype evolution?" does not appear in any intro.)
2. **Does not narrate the structure of the paper.** No "In
   Section 2 we review..." — sections speak for themselves.
3. **Does not announce novelty.** The word *novel* appears only
   in technical phrases like "novel Y chromosomes," never as
   self-praise.
4. **Does not use corporate connectives.** *Furthermore, Moreover,
   Additionally, In this regard* are rare. Paragraphs transition
   by content, not by adverbial scaffolding.
5. **Does not moralize.** No "This has important implications for
   society..." unless the paper is literally about that.
6. **Does not write single-sentence paragraphs for drama.** Every
   paragraph develops a point.
7. **Does not stack three synonyms.** One good word.
8. **Does not bury the finding.** The Discussion leads with it.

## How agents should use this file

1. **Load, don't mimic.** These rules describe how Heath writes
   when the content is *his*. Do not impose them on prose that
   another author is signing.
2. **Anchor to verbatim passages.** When stuck, scan the Anchor
   Passages above and match cadence against them, not against
   abstract rules.
3. **Prefer the rule, not the quote.** Do not paste his sentences
   into new manuscripts. The quotes are calibration anchors, not
   templates.
4. **Update over time.** This is a point-in-time calibration
   (see Provenance below). If Heath's recent writing diverges
   from this file, trust the recent writing and update this file.
5. **Pair with `prompts.json`.** The "coding-partner" prompt in
   `prompts.json` encodes Heath's voice for R collaboration
   (blunt, base-R, zero fluff). The present file encodes his
   voice for prose. They complement each other.
